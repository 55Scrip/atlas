UX-008 — Decision Workspace Philosophy

Status: Philosophy Complete
Owner: Atlas Product
Sequence: Dashboard → Investment Workspace → Portfolio Workspace → Decision Workspace
Depends on: UX-000 — The Atlas Experience, UX-006 — Portfolio Workspace Philosophy

⸻

Executive Definition

The Decision Workspace is a focused reasoning environment where the user forms, examines, documents, and preserves an investment decision.

It is the bridge between analysis and commitment. Analysis may be extensive. A decision must be explicit.

The Decision Workspace is not a trading interface, a recommendation feed, or a generic note-taking tool. It is the final reasoning layer before a decision becomes part of the user's investment history. Its function is to convert the conclusions developed in the Investment Workspace and Portfolio Workspace into a deliberate, explicit, reviewable decision — and to preserve the reasoning behind that decision in a form that remains understandable years from now.

The Decision Workspace is part of Atlas Memory.

⸻

What the Decision Workspace Is

* A focused reasoning environment for forming and finalizing investment decisions
* A bridge between analysis and commitment
* A place where the decision becomes explicit rather than implied
* A permanent record of the user's reasoning at the moment of decision
* A source of context for all future reviews, briefings, and thesis monitoring
* A contribution to Atlas Memory and the user's long-term decision pattern

What the Decision Workspace Is Not

* A brokerage interface or trade execution screen
* A place for impulsive action
* An AI-generated buy or sell command
* A recommendation feed or alert center
* A generic note-taking or journaling tool
* A post-hoc justification engine
* A substitute for the user's judgment
* A required step for every minor review

Atlas may help clarify the decision, expose contradictions, and challenge weak reasoning. The user remains responsible for the final decision. Atlas proposes. The user decides.

⸻

Purpose

The Decision Workspace exists because analysis without decision is incomplete, and decisions without reasoning are fragile.

Investment decisions routinely occur informally — in the margin of a research note, in a chat message to a broker, or in a mental commitment that never gets recorded. When that happens, the reasoning behind the decision disappears. Future reviews then have nothing to compare against. Self-knowledge as an investor cannot accumulate.

The Decision Workspace gives the decision itself a durable identity. It preserves not merely what was decided but why — and under what conditions the decision was considered reasonable.

⸻

Central User Question

The Decision Workspace should answer:

What have I decided, why have I decided it, and what must be true for that decision to remain valid?

It should not merely answer: What should I buy or sell?

The Workspace supports decisions of all types — action, inaction, deferral, and rejection. Doing nothing is a legitimate decision when it is deliberate and reasoned. The Workspace treats no action with the same seriousness as any allocation change.

1. Role Within the Atlas System

The Atlas reasoning model is sequential:

Dashboard
Identifies what deserves attention.

↓

Investment Workspace
Examines whether the user's judgment about an individual investment still holds.

↓

Portfolio Workspace
Examines whether the portfolio remains positioned in accordance with the user's objectives, convictions, risks, and capital-allocation priorities.

↓

Decision Workspace
Helps the user form, examine, document, and preserve an investment decision.

The Dashboard identifies the issue. The Investment or Portfolio Workspace develops the reasoning. The Decision Workspace resolves what the user intends to do.

The Decision Workspace should not repeat the complete analysis from previous Workspaces. It should preserve the small number of conclusions, assumptions, risks, and dependencies necessary to understand the decision later.

The resulting recorded decision feeds forward into:

* future Investment Workspace reviews — as context against which to measure change
* future Portfolio Workspace reviews — as the established rationale for each position and allocation
* Daily Briefings — to surface decisions that may need implementation or revisiting
* thesis monitoring — to track whether the conditions underlying the decision still hold
* assumption tracking — to record which beliefs were held at the time
* portfolio evolution — to show how decisions shaped the portfolio as a system
* decision reviews — to examine whether the decision remained appropriate
* outcome analysis — to evaluate the decision against eventual results without conflating outcome with quality
* pattern recognition — to identify recurring behavioral tendencies
* investor self-reflection — to support the user's long-term growth as a decision-maker

⸻

2. Decision as a First-Class Object

A decision is not a chat message, a transaction record, or an annotation. In Atlas, the investment decision is a first-class object — a discrete entity with a defined structure, a lifecycle, and a permanent identity.

A decision should not disappear into a transcript. It should remain understandable years after it was made, even without access to the market data, news, or conversations that surrounded it.

Every recorded decision should preserve:

What was decided — the commitment, clearly stated
What the decision applies to — the investment, position, or portfolio-level element
The date and context — when and under what conditions
The reason — the primary motivation for the decision
The supporting assumptions — what was believed to be true
The evidence relied upon — which conclusions from prior analysis were most relevant
The relevant risks — what might make the decision wrong
Invalidation conditions — what specific changes would make the decision no longer reasonable
Whether action is required — immediate, staged, conditional, or none
Implementation status — whether the decision has been acted upon
Review conditions — what should trigger the next formal examination

These dimensions matter not because they satisfy a form, but because they support the user's future question: What did I believe when I made this decision?

The Decision Workspace should make that question answerable.

⸻

3. Decision Types

Not every investment situation resolves to buy, hold, or sell. Those three labels are too coarse for careful long-term investment reasoning. The Decision Workspace should support a richer and more precise vocabulary.

Investment-Level Decisions

Initiate — begin a new position
Add — increase an existing position
Maintain — affirm the current position without change
Reduce — decrease a position with or without full exit
Exit — close a position entirely
Avoid — reject an opportunity and record the reason
Defer — decline to decide until a specified condition changes

Portfolio-Level Decisions

Reallocate capital — move capital from one investment to another
Reduce concentration — deliberately shrink a shared exposure
Increase theme exposure — add to a directional portfolio bet
Reduce a macro dependency — decrease vulnerability to a shared driver
Preserve liquidity — choose to hold capital rather than deploy it
Rebalance conviction — restore position sizes to their intended relationship with conviction
Accept a known concentration — consciously affirm an elevated exposure with full understanding
Maintain portfolio structure — affirm the current allocation without change

Review Decisions

Thesis remains valid — no action required, decision maintained
Thesis requires revision — the supporting case must be updated
Evidence is insufficient — decision postponed until more information is available
Decision postponed conditionally — action deferred until a named condition changes

The interface should not compress every situation into three labels. The depth of vocabulary available should reflect the genuine complexity of investment reasoning.

⸻

4. The Difference Between Conclusion and Decision

One of the most important conceptual distinctions in the Decision Workspace is the line between conclusion and decision.

A conclusion describes what Atlas and the user currently believe about the situation.

A decision describes what the user commits to doing, not doing, or reconsidering.

Examples:

Conclusion: LVMH remains a high-quality business, but the expected return has weakened and the position competes with stronger opportunities.
Decision: Reduce the position from 8% to 5% and reallocate the released capital to Danaher, subject to the current valuation remaining within the accepted range.

Conclusion: The thesis remains intact and no material assumption has changed.
Decision: Maintain the current position and review again after the next earnings report.

Conclusion: Evidence is insufficient to determine whether the thesis has broken.
Decision: Defer the decision. No action until the next earnings report clarifies the trend.

The Decision Workspace must make this distinction unmistakable. Analysis produces conclusions. The user produces decisions. The Workspace converts one into the other without collapsing the distinction.

⸻

5. Decision Quality Before Decision Speed

The Decision Workspace must optimize for decision quality rather than speed or activity.

The investor who acts faster is not a better investor. The investor who acts with clearer reasoning, better-identified assumptions, and stronger self-awareness makes better decisions over time.

The Workspace should build quality through:

Beginning with the current conclusion — never asking the user to reach a decision without first establishing what the analysis supports
Making the reason explicit — requiring the user to state why this decision is being made now
Surfacing unresolved contradictions — identifying where the proposed decision conflicts with prior reasoning or stated strategy
Showing what the decision depends on — making visible which conclusions and assumptions the decision relies upon
Distinguishing facts from assumptions — never allowing assumed conditions to appear as established facts
Exposing opportunity cost — making alternative uses of capital visible before a significant allocation decision
Showing portfolio consequences — connecting the individual decision to portfolio-level impact
Allowing deferral — treating the decision to wait as a valid and recorded outcome
Requiring clarity before completion — the Workspace should not allow a decision to be recorded if critical dimensions remain unaddressed

Deliberate friction is appropriate in certain moments:

* asking the user to state why the decision is being made now rather than after more information
* asking what would make the decision wrong
* showing conflicts with prior reasoning
* identifying whether the user is reacting to price movement without a thesis change
* distinguishing a temporary emotional reaction from a structural change
* noting when a proposed decision contradicts the portfolio strategy

This friction should be deliberate, not bureaucratic. Minor decisions should not require exhaustive documentation. Significant decisions — larger allocations, exits from core positions, portfolio-level reallocations — should engage the full depth of the Workspace. The depth of the process should match the significance of the decision.

⸻

6. Atlas's Role in the Decision

Atlas's role is to improve the quality of the user's reasoning. It is not to decide on the user's behalf.

Atlas should:

* Summarize the relevant reasoning from prior Workspaces — concisely, without restating the full analysis
* Identify the decision that appears to follow from the analysis — presented as a proposal, not a command
* Highlight inconsistencies — between the proposed decision and prior reasoning, stated strategy, or portfolio context
* Expose missing information — when the available evidence is insufficient to support a high-confidence decision
* Identify unsupported assumptions — beliefs that the user is treating as established facts
* Compare the proposed decision with prior decisions on the same investment — to surface consistency or contradiction
* Show portfolio-level consequences — how the decision changes allocation, concentration, or exposure
* Clarify opportunity cost — what is not being done as a result of this decision
* Help define review conditions — what Atlas should monitor after the decision is recorded
* Help formulate the decision precisely — supporting clear, specific language over vague commitments

Atlas should not:

* Present its judgment as unquestionable
* Conceal uncertainty or express false confidence
* Manufacture urgency unless urgency is genuinely present
* Convert uncertain projections into stated facts
* Make irreversible choices on behalf of the user
* Create a decision merely because the Workspace was opened
* Diagnose the user's emotional state using clinical or patronizing language
* Imply that analysis is complete when it is not

The final decision must visibly belong to the user.

⸻

7. Decision Narrative

The Decision Workspace should feel like a coherent reasoning sequence, not a form to complete.

A strong default narrative sequence:

Current Conclusion — what the analysis currently supports

↓

Why a Decision Is Required — the trigger: what changed, what is unresolved, what demands attention

↓

Proposed Decision — what the user is considering doing, stated explicitly

↓

Primary Reasoning — the most important reason behind the proposed decision

↓

What Supports It — the conclusions, evidence, and assumptions that make the decision reasonable

↓

What Challenges It — the counterarguments, uncertainties, and risks that could make the decision wrong

↓

Portfolio Consequences — how the decision changes the portfolio's position, allocation, and dependency profile

↓

Opportunity Cost — what is not being done as a result of this decision; what competing alternatives exist

↓

Conditions and Invalidation — what must remain true; what would make the decision no longer valid

↓

Final Decision — the explicit, stated commitment, including scope, implementation intent, and confidence level

↓

Review Plan — when and under what conditions the decision should be revisited

↓

Record Decision — the decision becomes part of the user's investment history

This narrative should be experienced as a progressive clarification. By the time the user reaches the final decision, it should feel earned — arrived at through structured reasoning rather than suggested abruptly. The Workspace should make the user feel more certain about their decision at the end than they were at the beginning, or more certain that deferral is the right choice.

⸻

8. Reasoning Depth and Preserved Context

The Decision Workspace does not copy the full analysis from prior Workspaces. Its role is to preserve the reasoning necessary to understand the decision — not the entire analysis that preceded it.

When revisiting a recorded decision years later, the user should be able to answer:

* Why was this decision made?
* What information mattered most?
* Which assumptions were uncertain at the time?
* Which alternatives were considered?
* What outcome was expected?
* What would have caused a different decision?

The information preserved should be selective but sufficient. The Workspace should guide the user toward capturing what is genuinely explanatory, not what is merely comprehensive.

⸻

9. Opportunity Cost and Capital Competition

Every investment decision involves an opportunity cost — the alternative use of capital that is foregone.

The Decision Workspace should make capital competition visible without turning every decision into an exhaustive optimization exercise.

For minor decisions — a routine review confirmation, a maintenance decision — opportunity cost may not require explicit treatment.

For significant allocation decisions, the Workspace should answer:

Why this use of capital rather than the available alternatives?

Examples of opportunity cost that deserve attention:

* Keeping capital in an existing position instead of initiating a stronger opportunity
* Adding to a position that already represents elevated concentration
* Reducing a position to fund a higher-expected-return alternative
* Holding cash as a deliberate alternative to deploying into an unattractive opportunity
* Maintaining a position whose opportunity cost has become material as alternatives have improved

The goal is not to maximize mechanical efficiency. The goal is to make the trade-off conscious and recorded.

⸻

10. Assumptions, Conditions, and Invalidation

Every meaningful decision rests on conditions that may later change. The Decision Workspace should distinguish three related but distinct concepts.

Supporting Assumptions
What the decision currently relies upon — the beliefs that make the decision reasonable under current conditions.

Monitoring Conditions
What Atlas should continue observing after the decision is recorded — the signals that indicate whether the decision remains valid.

Invalidation Conditions
What specific changes would make the original decision no longer reasonable — the thresholds at which the decision should be revisited or reversed.

Examples of invalidation conditions:

* Revenue growth falls below the range assumed in the thesis
* Leverage exceeds the level that justified the risk-adjusted return
* Management changes capital-allocation policy against the investor's expectations
* Valuation reaches a level where the expected return becomes uncompetitive
* A shared portfolio dependency becomes more concentrated than the investor intended
* The expected return falls below the best available alternative
* Evidence accumulates that contradicts the core thesis

Invalidation should be grounded in reasoning, evidence, and thesis integrity — not arbitrary price movement. A position declining 15% is not, by itself, an invalidation condition. A core assumption breaking is.

⸻

11. Decision Confidence and Uncertainty

The Decision Workspace must not create artificial precision.

Uncertainty is not a failure state. It is an honest condition that deserves explicit representation.

Avoid percentage confidence scores unless supported by rigorous evidence. Prefer clear qualitative language:

* High confidence — the reasoning is well-supported and the assumptions are well-established
* Moderate confidence — the reasoning is sound but depends on uncertain conditions
* Low confidence — the evidence is incomplete or the assumptions are contested
* Evidence incomplete — the available information is insufficient for a high-confidence decision
* Dependent on uncertain assumptions — the decision is reasonable but would change if key beliefs proved false
* Intentionally deferred — the decision is being postponed as a deliberate choice

Confidence describes the strength of the reasoning, not the probability of a positive investment outcome.

A decision may be recorded even when significant uncertainty remains, provided that uncertainty is explicit. Recording a decision with acknowledged uncertainty is better than refusing to decide until perfect information exists.

⸻

12. Decision States

Every decision exists in a lifecycle. The most useful conceptual states are:

Draft — the decision is being formed but not yet ready to record
Under Review — the reasoning is being examined or challenged
Ready to Record — the decision is explicit, its scope is clear, and the user is prepared to commit
Recorded — the decision has been formally documented in Atlas Memory
Implementation Pending — the decision has been recorded but not yet acted upon
Implemented — the intended action has been taken
Partially Implemented — the decision has been acted on in part
Superseded — a later decision has replaced this one
Cancelled — the decision was recorded but the user determined it should not be implemented
Due for Review — a monitoring condition or review date has been reached
Reviewed — the decision has been formally examined against current conditions

States that create unnecessary complexity — such as granular substates for each implementation step — should be avoided. The distinction that matters most is between:

* The decision itself — what was committed to, when, and why
* Implementation of the decision — whether the intended action was taken
* Later review of the decision — whether the reasoning remained appropriate given subsequent evidence
* The eventual outcome — whether the results aligned with expectations

This last distinction is fundamental to Atlas. A decision can be correct even if the outcome is negative. A decision can be poorly reasoned even if the outcome is positive. The Decision Workspace evaluates the quality of the reasoning, not the performance of the stock.

⸻

13. Decision Versus Implementation

A recorded decision and an executed transaction are not the same thing.

The user may decide to reduce a position but have not yet placed a trade. They may decide to initiate a position gradually over three months. They may decide to act only if a valuation condition is met. They may decide to take no action at all.

Atlas should track whether implementation occurred without becoming the execution venue.

The Decision Workspace may record:

Immediate action — the decision calls for action now
Staged action — the decision calls for execution across multiple steps or time periods
Conditional action — the decision calls for action if a named condition is met
No action — the decision is to maintain the current state deliberately
Deferred action — the decision and implementation are both postponed to a future condition

The Workspace must remain useful even when Atlas has no brokerage integration. Its purpose is to preserve the decision and its reasoning, not to execute it.

⸻

14. Preventing Post-Hoc Rationalization

One of the most important functions of the Decision Workspace is to preserve the user's reasoning before future outcomes can distort their memory of what they believed.

Humans are skilled at rewriting their own history. After a decision produces a poor outcome, they remember their uncertainty as greater than it was. After a decision produces a strong outcome, they remember their conviction as greater than it was. In both cases, the lesson is lost.

A recorded decision defeats this by preserving the original:

* Conclusion — what Atlas and the user believed the situation warranted
* Assumptions — what the user was treating as established
* Uncertainties — what the user acknowledged they did not know
* Expected consequences — what outcome the user was anticipating
* Alternatives considered — what other decisions were available
* Invalidation conditions — what would have caused a different decision
* Review horizon — when the user expected to return to this decision

Later edits must not silently overwrite the historical record. When the user's view changes, Atlas should record a new review, an amendment, or a superseding decision — not alter the original.

The system exists to support intellectual honesty. The user must be able to trust that the record of their past reasoning has not been quietly adjusted.

⸻

15. Decision Memory

Recorded decisions are the raw material for the most important long-term feature in Atlas: the user's self-knowledge as an investor.

Over time, Atlas should be able to identify patterns such as:

* Repeated overreaction to short-term price movement
* Reluctance to exit positions whose theses have clearly weakened
* Excessive confidence in a specific sector or type of thesis
* Recurring decisions that depended on the same assumption that repeatedly failed
* Strong decisions that were initially uncomfortable but proved correct
* Systematic opportunity-cost errors — missed alternatives that consistently offered better returns
* Successful use of patience — decisions to hold or defer that were eventually vindicated
* Premature action — acting before evidence was sufficient
* Delayed action — failing to act despite clear invalidation

The Decision Workspace should collect enough structured reasoning to support this future learning without making the current experience feel clinical or burdensome.

The user should gradually feel: Atlas understands not only my portfolio, but how I make decisions.

That understanding accumulates across decisions. The quality of that accumulation depends on the quality of what is recorded in each individual Decision Workspace.

⸻

16. Emotional and Behavioral Context

Investment decisions are affected by emotion, recent performance, conviction, fear, and urgency. The Decision Workspace may acknowledge this context where it materially improves the quality of the decision.

Conditions worth surfacing when present:

* Decisions made in response to a price decline that has not changed the thesis
* Decisions made after rapid appreciation that may have compressed the expected return
* Attachment to a long-held position that may be distorting the user's view of current evidence
* Reluctance to admit that a thesis has weakened
* Urgency created by recent news that may not represent a structural change
* Desire to recover a prior loss through a related decision
* Overconfidence following a period of strong recent success

This context should be raised calmly and respectfully. Atlas must not diagnose the user, use patronizing language, or assign emotional states without clear behavioral evidence. The goal is not to override the user's judgment but to ensure that emotional or behavioral factors do not invisibly dominate the decision without acknowledgment.

Behavioral context should be surfaced only when it is specific and material. It should never become a default framing for every decision.

⸻

17. Adaptive Depth

Not every decision requires the same amount of work. A routine maintenance confirmation does not need the same depth as an exit from a core position.

A minor review decision may require only:

* Current conclusion
* Decision
* Primary reason
* Next review condition

A significant capital reallocation may require:

* Competing alternatives considered
* Portfolio impact
* Supporting assumptions
* Material risks
* Invalidation conditions
* Implementation plan
* Review schedule

A full exit from a core position — especially one the user has held for years — may require the deepest record: the original thesis, how it evolved, what finally triggered the decision, what evidence would have produced a different outcome, and what the user would do differently in future decisions of this kind.

The Workspace should scale along dimensions including:

* Capital significance — larger decisions warrant deeper reasoning
* Portfolio impact — decisions that change the portfolio's character more substantially deserve more examination
* Uncertainty — higher uncertainty calls for more explicit acknowledgment
* Irreversibility — decisions that are harder to undo should face more deliberate friction
* Deviation from prior strategy — significant departures from established strategy deserve explicit justification
* Conflict with prior reasoning — when a decision contradicts something the user previously committed to, that conflict should be surfaced and resolved

The experience should remain lightweight when appropriate and rigorous when necessary.

⸻

18. Completion Standard

A decision is ready to record when:

* The decision is explicit — not implied, not deferred without reason
* Its scope is clear — what investment or portfolio element it applies to, and to what degree
* The primary reason is stated — the most important motivation for the decision, in the user's own terms
* Material uncertainty is visible — the user has acknowledged what they do not know
* Key assumptions are identified — the conditions that must remain true for the decision to be reasonable
* Portfolio implications are understood — for any significant allocation decision, the user has considered the portfolio-level effect
* Unresolved contradictions are acknowledged — if conflicts with prior reasoning exist, they are named rather than ignored
* Implementation status is defined — immediate, staged, conditional, deferred, or none
* Review or invalidation conditions are established — what would bring the user back to this decision

False certainty must not be required. Not every analytical question must be resolved. A valid completion state may be:

Defer the decision. The available evidence is insufficient. Review after the next earnings report.

That is still a decision. It is explicit, reasoned, and recorded.

⸻

19. Tone and Experiential Character

The Decision Workspace should feel:

Calm — unhurried, without manufactured urgency
Deliberate — structured to support careful reasoning rather than speed
Focused — organized around the decision at hand, without extraneous information
Serious without being intimidating — the weight of the decision should be apparent, but the experience should remain accessible
Premium — worthy of the significance of investment decisions
Intellectually honest — uncertainty visible, limitations acknowledged, alternatives represented
Restrained — no unnecessary elements, no decorative urgency
Clear — decisions and reasoning expressed in plain, precise language
Personal — the experience should feel like the user's reasoning workspace, not a generic product
Durable — the design should feel appropriate not only in the moment of decision but when revisited years later

The Decision Workspace should not feel:

Transactional — it must not resemble a brokerage interface
Urgent by default — urgency should appear only when genuinely warranted
Gamified — no streaks, scores, completion rewards, or celebratory states after decisions
Judgmental — Atlas may challenge reasoning, but must never criticize the user
Bureaucratic — long forms, excessive required fields, or unnecessary confirmation screens
Overloaded — the experience must not present more information than the decision requires
Clinical — the Workspace should feel like a reasoning environment, not a diagnostic tool

The act of recording a decision should feel meaningful — but not dramatic. The significance is in the reasoning, not the act.

20. Governing Principles

These principles govern every future Decision Workspace design and implementation decision.

1. The user owns the decision.
   Atlas may propose, clarify, and challenge. The final decision belongs entirely to the user. No Atlas output implies that a decision has been made without the user's explicit commitment.

2. Atlas improves reasoning, not activity.
   The Workspace is not optimized for frequency of decisions, speed of completion, or volume of changes. It is optimized for the quality of reasoning that precedes a decision.

3. No action is a valid decision.
   Maintaining a position, deferring a decision, or explicitly rejecting an opportunity are all legitimate outcomes of the Decision Workspace. They must be recorded with the same seriousness as allocation changes.

4. Conclusions and decisions are distinct.
   Analysis produces conclusions about the current situation. The user produces a decision about what to do. The Workspace converts one into the other without collapsing the distinction.

5. Every decision preserves its original context.
   A decision record must remain understandable years after it was made, without access to the surrounding market conditions, conversations, or research. The reasoning must be self-contained.

6. Uncertainty must remain visible.
   The Decision Workspace must not create false precision. Uncertainty that was present at the time of the decision must be visible in the record. Confidence should describe the strength of reasoning, not predict investment outcomes.

7. Opportunity cost matters.
   For every significant allocation decision, the Workspace must make visible what the user is not doing with the same capital. Capital competition should be explicit, not implicit.

8. Decisions and transactions are separate.
   A recorded decision is not an executed trade. The Workspace tracks what was decided and whether implementation occurred. It does not execute anything.

9. Historical reasoning must never be silently rewritten.
   When the user's view changes, a new review or superseding decision must be recorded. The original decision must remain intact and retrievable. Post-hoc rationalization is a systematic risk that the Workspace must structurally resist.

10. The depth of the process should match the significance of the decision.
    Minor decisions warrant lightweight documentation. Major decisions warrant rigorous examination. The Workspace must scale with capital significance, portfolio impact, uncertainty, irreversibility, and deviation from prior strategy.

11. The final decision must be explicit, reviewable, and durable.
    Vague intentions, implied commitments, and general dispositions are not decisions. The recorded decision must be clear enough to evaluate later against both subsequent evidence and eventual outcomes.

12. Every recorded decision should make future decisions better.
    The Decision Workspace is not only a record of what the user decided. It is the foundation of the user's long-term growth as an investor. The reasoning preserved today is the material that Atlas uses to help the user recognize their patterns, correct their errors, and compound their wisdom.

What UX-008 Establishes

The following questions are now conceptually settled:

— What the Decision Workspace is: a focused reasoning environment and permanent record of the user's investment decision, not a trading interface or recommendation feed.

— What problem it solves: the disappearance of investment reasoning between analysis and action, and the inability to evaluate past decisions fairly without a preserved record.

— Where it sits in Atlas: the final reasoning layer, downstream of the Investment Workspace and Portfolio Workspace, feeding forward into Atlas Memory, thesis monitoring, and decision pattern recognition.

— The central user question: What have I decided, why have I decided it, and what must be true for that decision to remain valid?

— What constitutes a decision: a broad vocabulary of investment-level, portfolio-level, and review decisions, including no action and deferral as full legitimate outcomes.

— The distinction between conclusion and decision: conclusions describe the current situation; decisions describe what the user commits to.

— Atlas's role: to improve reasoning, not to substitute for the user's judgment. Atlas proposes. The user decides.

— What every decision must preserve: scope, reason, assumptions, uncertainties, alternatives considered, invalidation conditions, implementation status, and review conditions.

— The decision lifecycle: draft through recorded, implementation tracking, review, and the distinction between a decision's quality and its outcome.

— How uncertainty should be expressed: qualitative confidence language, not numerical scores. Uncertainty must remain explicit and visible.

— How opportunity cost should be addressed: visible for significant allocation decisions without becoming exhaustive optimization.

— How the Workspace should prevent post-hoc rationalization: by preserving original reasoning intact and requiring new records for changes rather than silent edits.

— How depth should adapt: scaled to capital significance, portfolio impact, uncertainty, irreversibility, and deviation from prior reasoning.

— The completion standard: a decision is ready when it is explicit, scoped, reasoned, honest about uncertainty, and has defined review conditions.

— The experiential character: calm, deliberate, serious, premium, honest, and personal — not transactional, urgent, gamified, or bureaucratic.

— The twelve governing principles that must apply to every future implementation decision.

What UX-008 Does Not Yet Specify

The following are intentionally deferred to later documents:

— Screen structure and layout: where the sections appear, how the page is divided, and what proportion of space each element occupies.

— Component design: the specific buttons, inputs, cards, fields, or interaction patterns that will implement the philosophy.

— Interaction model: how the user navigates through the decision sequence, what expands and collapses, and what triggers what.

— Navigation and Workspace entry: how the user opens the Decision Workspace, from which Atlas surface, and under which conditions.

— Visual design: typography, color, spacing, density, and visual hierarchy.

— Responsive and adaptive behavior: how the Workspace behaves across screen sizes and devices.

— Atlas-proposed decision language: the exact phrasing Atlas uses to surface a proposed decision.

— Friction implementation: the specific moments where deliberate friction appears and the exact form it takes.

— The relationship between the Decision Workspace and the Investment Workspace shell: whether the Decision Workspace appears within an existing overlay or as a distinct surface.

— Keyboard navigation, accessibility standards, and motion behavior.

— Data model and persistence: how decisions are stored, structured, and queried.

Requirements for UX-009

The Decision Workspace Screen Specification must answer:

1. How does the user enter the Decision Workspace? From which Atlas surfaces? Under which conditions is the Workspace offered or required?

2. What is the surface structure of the Workspace? Is it a new overlay, a section within an existing Workspace, or a distinct page?

3. How is the decision narrative presented visually? Is the sequence linear, accordion-style, progressive, or some combination?

4. What does the user see first? How is the current conclusion presented, and how prominently?

5. How does Atlas surface a proposed decision? What form does the proposal take, and how clearly is it labeled as Atlas's proposal rather than a commitment?

6. How does the user state and refine the decision? Is there a text field, a structured form, a constrained vocabulary, or a combination?

7. How is opportunity cost shown for significant allocation decisions? What level of detail is appropriate without becoming exhaustive?

8. How are supporting assumptions captured? Are they free-form, structured prompts, or both?

9. How are invalidation conditions expressed? Are they free-form, templated, or drawn from the investment thesis?

10. How is confidence communicated? Is it user-stated, Atlas-inferred, or both? What are the visible states?

11. What does the Record Decision action look like? How does it feel distinct from an ordinary button press?

12. How is the decision presented after it is recorded? What does the confirmed state look like?

13. How are draft and incomplete decisions handled? What happens when the user navigates away before recording?

14. How does the Workspace handle the distinction between the decision and its implementation? Where does implementation status appear?

15. What is the review plan interface? How does the user set conditions, dates, or triggers for future review?

16. How does the Workspace scale between lightweight review decisions and full exit decisions? Is there a mode switch, adaptive layout, or progressive disclosure?
