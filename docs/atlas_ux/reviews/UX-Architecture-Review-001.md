# UX Architecture Review 001

## 1. Executive Assessment

**Overall architectural grade: C+ (Conditionally Ready).** The philosophy layer is unusually strong and disciplined; the mid-tier design-system layer contains at least one core contradiction serious enough to misdirect an entire Workspace's visual priorities; and the component-library layer's most "final" document rests in significant part on two source specifications (UX-013C, UX-013D) that are not present in the committed set and cannot be independently verified.

**Implementation readiness:** Foundation-tier components (UX-013A) are close to buildable as-is. Reasoning-tier components (UX-013B) are buildable with caution around several named naming/severity conflicts. Decision, Monitoring, and AI-Collaboration-tier components are **not** independently verifiable from the committed source set — their only specification lives inside UX-013E's secondhand account of documents this repository does not contain.

**Documentation reliability:** High in volume and intention, uneven in internal consistency. Multiple documents contain internal self-contradictions (a document that states "fourteen" states in prose but lists thirteen in its own table; a document whose own self-audit claims full alignment with an upstream spec that a fresh read does not support). This matters more than any individual defect: it means no single document in this set can be trusted as self-consistent without independent verification, which is itself a governance finding, not a wording nitpick.

**Highest-risk issue:** UX-012 and UX-012A both present themselves as "the six-level information hierarchy" and are both included, unflagged as mutually exclusive, in the committed set — yet they define Levels 2, 4, and 5 differently, most consequentially over whether Challenges/contradiction content is a high-priority, must-not-be-buried tier (UX-012A) or effectively unplaced, generic "Contextual Information" (UX-012). Atlas's own stated product identity — honest reasoning under uncertainty, conclusions that do not overstate certainty — depends on this exact decision. Two teams building from different documents in this same committed set would build products with a different relationship to their own counter-evidence. This is not a stylistic disagreement; it is a contradiction at the level the whole product's credibility rests on.

**Single most important next action:** Before any Decision-Workspace-tier or cross-Workspace component work begins, resolve — as one explicit, dated architectural decision, not a silent implementation choice — (a) which information-hierarchy definition governs (UX-012 or UX-012A) and where Challenges sits within it, (b) the correct canonical Decision Workspace section order (UX-009A, UX-012, and UX-013B each give a different sequence), and (c) the Record-Decision completion-gate field count (UX-009A says four required fields; UX-012 and UX-012C say two). None of these three is a matter of taste; each is a fact about the product that the documentation set currently states more than one way.

A high grade is not being withheld here because the documents are short or the coverage is thin — the opposite is true; several individual documents exceed 2,000 lines of granular, well-considered specification. The grade reflects that detail and internal consistency are different properties, and this set has a great deal of the first and a material deficit of the second at exactly the layers (information hierarchy, Decision Workspace structure, component provenance) where getting it wrong is most expensive to unwind.

## 3. Document Architecture

The eighteen documents divide cleanly into five real layers, and the layering itself is one of the set's genuine strengths:

1. **Product/experience philosophy** — UX-000 (Atlas overall), UX-004 (Investment Workspace), UX-008 (Decision Workspace). No screens, no components, no visual language. Each explicitly refuses to specify layout: UX-008 closes with "What UX-008 Does Not Yet Specify," listing screen structure, component design, interaction model, and visual design as future work.
2. **Screen/information-architecture specification** — UX-005, UX-009. Explicitly excludes visual styling: UX-009's metadata states "No wireframe is produced here. No visual styling is specified," with its own closing section "What UX-009 Defers to UX-009A."
3. **Wireframe/structural specification** — UX-007A, UX-009A. Component hierarchy, section behavior, editing ownership — still monochrome/low-fidelity by explicit instruction (UX-007A: "This document does not define: - final colors, - final typography, - decorative illustration...").
4. **Interaction and visual-design specification** — UX-010, UX-011, UX-007P. Microinteractions, motion, typography, color semantics.
5. **Cross-Workspace design system and component library** — UX-012 (and its four superseded parts UX-012A–D), UX-013A, UX-013B, UX-013E.

**Finding 3.1 — The layer separation is real and is respected in each document's own stated scope, not merely asserted.**
*Severity:* None (strength). *Evidence:* Every document in layers 1–4 contains an explicit, itemized "what this document does not specify" or "defers to" section, and the *next* document in the chain begins by explicitly citing the previous one as its sole input (UX-009: "Using the approved UX-008 Decision Workspace Philosophy as the governing document..."). *Consequence:* A reader can trust that visual detail will not leak backward into philosophy, and product-scope decisions will not leak forward into visual polish. This is the single best-executed structural property of the set. *Recommendation:* Preserve this discipline explicitly in any future Workspace's documentation — it is worth naming as a house standard, not just an emergent pattern.

**Finding 3.2 — The "final assembly" pattern (one document per sub-part, then one document that reconciles and supersedes them) is applied twice, with different naming outcomes each time, and the difference is itself evidence of drift in the documentation process.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* UX-012D's own forward section anticipates its assembler as "UX-012E" ("UX-012E is the final document in the UX-012 series... It supersedes UX-012A, UX-012B, UX-012C, and UX-012D as individual documents"), but the document that was actually produced and committed is named plainly **UX-012**, reusing the series number rather than following the A→E pattern UX-012D itself set up. The 013 series *did* follow that pattern (013A, 013B, [013C], [013D], 013E). *Architectural consequence:* a reader relying on any cross-reference that names "UX-012E" (there are several, quoted in Section 4) will not find a file by that name; they must already know that "UX-012" is the assembled document to make the connection. This is a real discoverability cost, not merely cosmetic — it is exactly the kind of drift that compounds as the series grows past two design-system generations. *Recommendation:* Adopt one fixed naming rule for "assembled/final" documents (either always reuse the series number, or always use the next letter) and apply it retroactively via a single renaming pass, not silently going forward.

**Finding 3.3 — Forward references between documents in the same lineage do not reliably match the title of the document that was actually produced.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* UX-009A's own metadata states `Defers to: UX-010 — Decision Workspace Visual Design`; the actual UX-010 is titled "Decision Workspace Interaction & Microinteraction Specification" (visual design is UX-011's subject). UX-012B's own closing section anticipates `UX-012C — Atlas Design System Interaction, Motion & System Behaviors`; the actual UX-012C is titled "Interaction, Navigation & Responsive Behavior." *Architectural consequence:* these are small individually, but they are evidence that the documents were not cross-verified against each other's final titles after being written — a process gap, not a content gap. If it happened twice in this set, it will recur in future Workspace documentation unless the process itself changes. *Recommendation:* A lightweight, mechanical pre-commit check (does every "Depends on/Defers to/Governs" reference match an actual, correctly-titled file in the set) would have caught both instances at zero design cost.

**Finding 3.4 — Nothing in the committed set is missing a governing philosophy layer except Portfolio Workspace, and that gap is structurally different from a normal "not yet written" gap.**
*Severity:* High (elaborated fully in Section 6 and Section 18). *Evidence:* UX-007A and UX-007P both explicitly *depend on* UX-006 and UX-007 rather than substituting for them (UX-007A: "Use UX-007 for behavioral requirements. Use UX-006 for portfolio philosophy."). Unlike the Decision Workspace lineage, where each document is a strict, self-contained refinement of the one before it, Portfolio Workspace's two committed documents (Wireframe, Final Polish) are refinements of a base that does not exist in this repository. *Consequence:* Portfolio Workspace's committed documentation is architecturally sound in isolation but cannot be verified as internally consistent with its own philosophy or screen architecture, because those documents are absent, not merely superseded.

**Finding 3.5 — No document duplicates another's *purpose* within a layer; duplication in this set occurs almost entirely at the component/state/token level (Sections 8, 9, 15), not at the document-architecture level.** *Severity:* None (strength, stated for completeness). This is a meaningfully different failure mode than most design-system audits find, and it is worth crediting explicitly: whoever structured the document hierarchy understood layering. The people (or process) that populated the component/token layer within that hierarchy did not maintain the same discipline — this asymmetry is itself informative and returns throughout this review.

## 4. Authority and Supersession Audit

**The clean case — UX-012 vs. UX-012A–D.** UX-012's own opening text: *"Version 1.0 — Final Governing Document... All prior part documents (012A–D) are superseded by this assembled version."* This is anticipated (if slightly mis-numbered, see Finding 3.2) by UX-012D itself. No document in the set contradicts this supersession, and downstream consumers (UX-013A, UX-013B) cite UX-012 alone, never UX-012A–D, as their governing reference. **This supersession claim is fully internally consistent and is the strongest authority chain in the set.**

**The partially-clean case — UX-013E vs. UX-013A and UX-013B.** UX-013E's own text: *"It supersedes UX-013A through UX-013D as the single governing component-level reference... Where UX-013A and UX-013E conflict on any of these governance matters, UX-013E governs."* — but scoped precisely: UX-013E supersedes UX-013A/013B only "as the governing authority for" names, classifications, dependency relationships, and composition rules; it explicitly states each source volume "remains the detailed specification for each ... Component." This is a well-drawn distinction (authority over *classification*, not full replacement of *content*) and is source-supported, not asserted by this review. **Neither UX-013A nor UX-013B anticipates this supersession in its own text** — both present themselves as complete, standalone, production-ready volumes with no forward awareness of a reconciling document. This is not a defect (UX-012A–D show the same asymmetry, and it is a normal feature of an "assemble later" process), but it does mean a reader of UX-013A or UX-013B alone would reasonably believe those documents to be final.

**The unverifiable case — UX-013E vs. absent UX-013C and UX-013D. Treated here, per the task's own instruction, as a major audit condition.**

UX-013E does not merely mention UX-013C/013D in passing — it builds substantial, specific portions of its own "canonical" model directly on top of them, attributing precise counts, prop names, and normalization decisions to documents this repository does not contain:

> "UX-013C — Atlas Component Specification: Decision & Monitoring Components / Establishes DecisionProposal, DecisionCard (7 lifecycle variants normalized to one component in this document), DecisionSummary, RecordedDecision, DecisionRationaleRef, DecisionHistory, DecisionAmendment, DecisionSupersession, DecisionOutcome, MonitoringCondition, MonitoringTrigger, ReviewTrigger, InvalidationCondition, ScheduledReview, ReviewSummary, ReviewOutcome, FollowUp, ImplementationPlan, ImplementationStatus, OutcomeTracking, TimelineEntry..."
> "UX-013D — ... Establishes AtlasSuggestion, AtlasInsight, AtlasQuestion, AtlasClarification, AtlasWarning, AIGeneratedSummary, AIAuthorshipIndicator, suggestion action contracts (Accept, Partially Accept, Reject, Dismiss, Restore, Explain, Compare)..."

Three-way distinction, as the task requires:

1. **Content independently supported by other committed documents.** The *broad existence and purpose* of most named items is independently derivable from UX-012's own component inventory (Sections 25–28), which names Proposed Decision, Final Decision Card, Decision History, Decision Amendment, Monitoring Condition, Review Trigger, Invalidation Trigger, Historical Record/Decision/Review, Atlas Suggestion, Atlas Insight, Atlas Warning, and Atlas Recommendation as real, purposeful components with their own one-paragraph definitions. So: *that Atlas has a Monitoring Condition concept, a Historical Decision concept, an Atlas Warning concept* — this is corroborated.
2. **Content stated only by UX-013E.** Every specific engineering detail is not corroborated anywhere else: the exact component counts ("~27 Decision and Monitoring types," "~35 AI, Metadata, and System types"), every specific variant count (7 DecisionCard lifecycle variants, 12 EmptyState subtypes, 6 AtlasWarning variants), every specific prop/API name (`isHistorical`, `lifecycleState`, `monitoringConditionId`), and every specific merge/normalization decision ("Historical Decision is now DecisionCard with `isHistorical={true}`," "AI Working State is now a behavior pattern," "Notification Center is confirmed Deferred"). A direct search across all 17 other committed documents for the exact strings `MetadataBlock`, `SourceReference`, `AtlasWarning` (as a component name), `StatusPresentation`, `SkeletonState`, and `AIWorkingState` returns zero matches outside UX-013E.
3. **Content that claims provenance from a missing source and therefore cannot be source-verified.** Everything in category 2 above falls here by definition: UX-013E's own attestation ("The library preserves the meaning established in UX-013A through UX-013D, confirmed by the reconciliation summary in Section 1 and the naming audit in Section 4") is UX-013E vouching for itself against a source only UX-013E can see. There is no independent check available anywhere in this repository.

**A further, sharper finding: the documents disagree with each other about whether UX-013C even existed yet at the time of writing.** UX-013B's own closing instruction reads: *"Do not produce UX-013C yet. The completed UX-013B is the prerequisite."* This states, at the time UX-013B was written, that UX-013C did not yet exist. UX-013E, written later, treats UX-013C as a completed prior volume it assembled from. Both of these statements can be true in sequence (UX-013C could have been produced between UX-013B and UX-013E and simply never captured in this export) — but this review cannot determine that, and states plainly that it cannot. **This is exactly the kind of provenance gap that must not be papered over by assuming good faith on either side.**

**UX-013E's own downstream-consumer claims about the same absent volumes appear inside UX-013A and UX-013B, written before UX-013E existed:** UX-013A: "Full-screen editing mode for authoring components (see Long-Form Editor spec in UX-013D)." UX-013B: "Editing: Long-Form Editor behavior applies (see UX-013D)." Both point forward to specification content in a document absent from this set, meaning even the *earlier*, non-final documents in this series already depended on content this review cannot verify.

**UX-007A/UX-007P vs. bare UX-007 — the relationship the documents actually state, not the one that might be assumed.** Neither document claims to replace UX-007. Checked directly, by full-text search, in both files: zero occurrences of "supersede" or "replace" applied to UX-007 in either document. UX-007A instead states the opposite relationship explicitly: *"UX-007A should refine and formalize this implementation rather than replace its central interaction model,"* and its own closing instruction reads *"Use UX-007 for behavioral requirements. Use UX-006 for portfolio philosophy."* **UX-007A and UX-007P actively assume UX-006 and UX-007 continue to exist and to be the governing reference for anything outside their own narrow (wireframe / polish) scope.** This is the single most consequential authority-chain fact in the set, because it means the two committed Portfolio Workspace documents are, by their own admission, incomplete without documents this repository does not have.

**No circular authority was found anywhere in the set.** Every Depends-on/Governs/Defers-to/supersession chain traced runs strictly forward in time and never loops back on itself. This is worth stating as a clean result, not a gap.

**Internal reference mismatches (documents citing something the set doesn't contain, beyond the four confirmed-absent documents):** UX-000 depends on "Atlas Product Vision v1.0" (outside the UX-0xx series entirely, absent); UX-005 depends on "UX-003 – The Atlas Working Session" (absent). Neither is flagged elsewhere in this review's severity table because neither sits on the Decision Workspace / component-library critical path this review is scoped to, but both should be tracked in the same missing-document register as UX-006/007/013C/013D (Section 18) rather than left as orphaned citations.

## 6. Workspace Architecture Audit

**Dashboard.** Purpose is stated with unusual clarity and is consistent everywhere it appears: *"The Dashboard immediately communicates what deserves attention. Nothing else."* (UX-000) It explicitly does not host editing, a full Conclusion, or Challenges/Assumptions containers — confirmed directly by UX-013E's own Workspace Coverage Matrix, which marks those "Not used" for Dashboard. This is the cleanest Workspace boundary in the set.

**Investment Workspace.** Purpose, entry, exit, and non-duplication are all explicitly stated in UX-004 with a genuinely strong exclusion principle: *"It is not a company profile. It is not a research terminal... Information is included because it matters to the current Investment Case. Everything else is excluded."* It produces a Conclusion, does not itself finalize a Decision, and explicitly hands off to the Decision Workspace ("The Investment Workspace surfaces the 'Record Decision' call-to-action," UX-009). This Workspace's philosophy-to-screen-spec lineage (UX-004 → UX-005) is internally consistent.

**Portfolio Workspace — evidence gap, treated per the task's own instruction.** Purpose is knowable only from the two present documents (UX-007A, UX-007P), both of which are explicit that they refine, not define, the Workspace's information architecture: *"Refine the existing Portfolio Workspace implementation without changing its established information architecture, section order, reasoning model, or interaction pattern... Do not add new features... Do not redesign the Workspace shell"* (UX-007P). **This review does not, and cannot, state what the Portfolio Workspace's entry/exit conditions, inherited context, or non-duplication boundary are at a philosophy level**, because UX-006 is absent; it can only state what the wireframe/polish layer assumes those to already be. This is not a gap this review can fill by inference from UX-007A/007P's content, and it has not been filled that way.

**Decision Workspace.** The most thoroughly specified Workspace in the set, with an explicit five-entry-point model (UX-009: from Investment Workspace, from Portfolio Workspace, from Dashboard, from a prior decision record, user-initiated), explicit context inheritance ("Entry carries context. When the Decision Workspace opens, it inherits the current conclusion, the originating Workspace, and the decision trigger" — UX-009), and an unambiguous statement that it never produces its own primary Conclusion, only consumes one, read-only. Decision ownership is completely unambiguous and is the strongest single ownership statement anywhere in the set: *"The Record Decision action cannot be triggered by Atlas"* (UX-009). This is exactly the kind of clear, testable, unambiguous ownership rule a design system should have at its most safety-critical action — and it is a genuine strength, undercut only by the section-order and completion-gate contradictions detailed in Section 7.

**Monitoring and Historical Review — not Workspaces, and the set is honest about this, though inconsistently so.** Neither is granted a dedicated Workspace anywhere in the committed documents. Monitoring exists only as a cross-cutting component *category* embedded in Dashboard/Investment/Decision Workspaces. Historical Review exists, inconsistently, as **a mode of the Decision Workspace** in the implementation-facing documents (UX-009A: *"Review Mode... The Workspace opens in review mode"*) but is capitalized once, in UX-012 §22/24, as if it were its own distinct surface (*"Review Conclusion... Position: top of Historical Review, displayed alongside the original Decision"*). UX-012 itself (§18/69) names *"Review Workspace"* among *"Three anticipated future Workspace types (not designed here),"* which is the honest, correct framing — but the stray capitalized "Historical Review" usage a few sections earlier in the same document contradicts that honesty by implying a surface that, per UX-012's own later admission, does not yet exist.

**Overlap and boundary discipline is real but partial.** UX-004's exclusion principle for Investment Workspace (quoted above) and UX-012D's explicit anti-pattern #5 (*"Dashboard Thinking in Reasoning Workspaces"*) show a genuine, repeated concern with keeping Workspaces from bleeding into each other. No equivalent explicit non-duplication statement was found anywhere in the set for Portfolio Workspace specifically against the Dashboard — the closest analogues are the general anti-pattern and Investment Workspace's own principle, applied by inference, not by a stated rule for Portfolio Workspace itself.

**Scalability to future Workspace types.** UX-012 itself names three anticipated future Workspaces (Review, Research, Scenario) without designing them — an appropriately deferred, non-blocking statement. The Workspace *frame* (Header, Footer, Navigation, Return Navigation) is specified generically enough in UX-013A that a fifth Workspace could plausibly reuse it without new Foundation-tier work. The place this will strain first is not the frame — it is the **six-level information hierarchy** (Section 7) and the **Reasoning component family** (Section 8), both of which were derived from, and are heavily shaped by, the Decision Workspace's specific reasoning shape (Conclusion → Supporting Factors → Challenges → Decision). A genuinely different future Workspace (e.g., a Scenario Workspace, already anticipated) may not map cleanly onto that shape, and nothing in the set discusses how the hierarchy or Reasoning components would need to flex for a Workspace whose primary output isn't a Decision.

## 7. Information Hierarchy Audit

This is the review's most consequential single finding, and it is stated here in full because Sections 3, 4, and 21 all depend on it.

**Two documents, two different six-level hierarchies, both presented as universal and neither flagged as provisional relative to the other:**

**UX-012 (the document that explicitly supersedes UX-012A):**
> Level 1 — Primary Conclusion. Level 2 — **Structural Element** (section headings, category labels — a purely typographic/structural role). Level 3 — Supporting Narrative. Level 4 — **Contextual Information** (timestamps, sources, metadata, annotations). Level 5 — Reference Content (historical labels, collapsed previews). Level 6 — System Metadata.

**UX-012A (the document UX-012 explicitly supersedes, but which is nonetheless committed alongside it, unflagged):**
> Level 1 — Primary conclusion or decision. Level 2 — **Material implication** (why the primary conclusion matters — full content-bearing narrative, not a structural label). Level 3 — Supporting reasoning. Level 4 — **Challenges, uncertainty, or contradiction** ("This level receives significant design attention"; "it must be visible and readable, not buried or suppressed"). Level 5 — Reference detail (evidence, sources, historical records, assumptions). Level 6 — System metadata.

**Why this is not a stylistic difference.** Level 2 differs in kind, not degree: UX-012's Level 2 is a label-only role with no content-emphasis meaning; UX-012A's Level 2 is a full narrative-emphasis tier ranked immediately below the Conclusion itself. Level 4 differs even more consequentially: UX-012A places Challenges/contradiction at explicit, defended high priority; UX-012's Level 4 is generic, metadata-grade "Contextual Information," and **UX-012's own Section 5 never uses the words "challenges" or "contradiction" at all** — meaning that under UX-012's hierarchy, read literally, Challenges has no stated level at all.

**This is not resolved by supersession alone, and downstream evidence is genuinely mixed.** UX-013B's own stated usage aligns with UX-012's version (placing metadata/labels at Levels 4–5), which would seem to settle the question in UX-012's favor by precedent — except that this reading requires Challenges to fall outside the six named levels entirely, which cannot be the intended outcome for a component family whose own documents describe Challenges as load-bearing to the product's honesty (see Section 10). **No document in the set states, in so many words, "Challenges sits at Level N" under UX-012's own hierarchy.** This is a genuine, unresolved gap, not merely a supersession technicality.

**Three additional hierarchy systems coexist without a stated conversion rule between any of them:**
- UX-009/UX-011's **3-tier "Reading Hierarchy"** (Highest/Medium/Low emphasis), local to the Decision Workspace, never explicitly mapped onto either six-level system.
- UX-004's informal 4-step progressive-disclosure chain (Conclusion → Key explanation → Detailed reasoning → Underlying evidence), predating and unreconciled with either six-level model.
- UX-005's fixed 11-section reading order for the Investment Workspace, which gives Challenges **no dedicated section at all** (folded into "Evidence & Assumptions"), a structural choice never explained relative to the Decision Workspace's later documents, which always give Challenges its own numbered section.

**Could design and engineering implement one hierarchy today without choosing between conflicting specifications? No.** A team following only UX-012 would treat Challenges as a matter of ordinary body-text or metadata styling. A team following UX-012A would give Challenges its own defended visual-priority tier. Both readings are textually supported; neither document acknowledges the other's existence in this specific respect. This is a **Critical** finding (formalized in Section 21, Finding C-1) precisely because the two candidate answers are not close to each other — they are opposite emphasis directions for the exact content (counter-evidence, uncertainty) that this product's own philosophy documents (UX-000, UX-004, UX-008) repeatedly identify as central to its value proposition.

## 8. Component Architecture Audit

**Semantic boundaries and taxonomy.** UX-012's glossary defines exactly two structural tiers — Component and Pattern. UX-013E replaces this, at final assembly, with a **ten-type classification model** (Primitive, Component, Composite Component, Action, Behavior, State, Variant, Composed Pattern, Semantic Concept, Deferred Item) that is never previewed anywhere upstream. This is not inherently wrong — a mature component library needs more than two categories — but it means the taxonomy the whole library is finally organized under was decided at the very last stage, with no chance for UX-012A–D or UX-013A/B to have been written against it. UX-013E's own explicit rules ("Actions are not automatically components," "States are not automatically variants") are good, precise engineering discipline; they simply arrive four documents later than the components they govern.

**Finding 8.1 — Duplicate/conflicting definitions of the same component.** (See Section 5 for the terminology dimension of these; this section states the component-architecture consequence.)
- **Recommendation** (Finding 5.3): two documents describe what reads as two different components before UX-013E merges them. *Consequence:* absent UX-013E, an implementer would build both.
- **Opportunity Cost/Summary** (Finding 5.5): three-way name/classification conflict.
- **Decision History vs. Historical Timeline Entry:** both are chronological-list constructs; UX-013E itself has to formally adjudicate this exact pair ("architecturally distinct... after applying a formal classification test"), which is itself evidence the overlap was real, not imagined.
- **Confidence** (Finding 5.4): three specificity levels for one component.

**Finding 8.2 — A component family with no antecedent, alongside a self-audit claim that is not accurate.**
*Severity:* Medium-High. *Evidence:* UX-013A introduces WorkspaceToolbar, Navigation Bar/Breadcrumb, Dialog Container, and the full Layout Container family (Column/Row/Stack/Split/Adaptive/Grid) with no corresponding named entry in UX-012's "Initial Atlas Component Inventory." UX-013A's own closing self-audit states: *"All sixteen Foundation Components are present in the UX-012 Component Inventory or are derived from UX-012 Workspace and Section specifications. No Foundation Component introduced in UX-013A conflicts with a UX-012 design decision."* **This claim does not hold up against a direct check of UX-012's own inventory list**, which contains none of the four component families above by name. *Consequence:* this is not merely a missing-antecedent problem (new components are a normal, healthy part of moving from Design System to Component Library); it is that the document's own verification claim is false, which means the self-audit process that produced it cannot be relied on without independent re-checking — exactly the discipline this review had to apply manually. *Recommendation:* treat every "alignment confirmed" or "no conflicts found" self-audit statement anywhere in this set as a claim to be spot-checked, not a fact to be trusted; this review found at least one such claim (this one) to be false on direct inspection.

**Finding 8.3 — Overlapping components never fully reconciled even by the document whose job is reconciliation.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* Scenario Analysis (new, UX-013B) and Scenario Comparison (a Comparison Type tracing to UX-012) both structure "potential outcomes under different conditions" as outcome-labeled cards/columns. UX-013B's own "Remaining Reasoning Questions" acknowledges ambiguity only relative to a *future* Scenario Workspace, not to its sibling Comparison-type in the same document. UX-013E's duplicate-component audit asserts the two are *"semantically distinct (evaluation of options vs. exploration of uncertain futures)"* without addressing the Scenario-Comparison-type-within-Comparison overlap specifically. *Consequence:* likely component consolidation debate during implementation that the documentation does not currently give engineers or designers grounds to resolve on their own.

**Finding 8.4 — Composite ownership and cross-package dependencies are, where stated, stated well.**
*Severity:* None (strength). *Evidence:* UX-012B is explicit and specific about what the Final Decision Card, Monitoring Condition, and Atlas Suggestion each depend on and cannot exist without ("The Atlas Suggestion depends on: the Long-form Editor or Short Statement it addresses. It cannot appear independently of a field context."). UX-013E extends this into a clean, explicitly-stated, ID-reference (not nested-import) dependency model between Reasoning and Decision components, and its own risk register names the highest-risk instance (Assumption→MonitoringCondition) by name. This is good architectural hygiene, consistently applied where it appears.

**Finding 8.5 — State-explosion candidates are real, but the set is self-aware about at least the largest one.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* DecisionCard normalizes seven prior "separate components" into one component behind a `lifecycleState` enum; UX-013E's own risk register flags this by name with a concrete mitigation ("Hard cap: maximum 48 variants per component set; decompose if exceeded"). EmptyState grew from 4 subtypes (UX-013A) to 12 (attributed to the absent UX-013D, so unverifiable — see Section 18). *Consequence:* the DecisionCard risk is disclosed and mitigated in the text itself, which is the correct posture; the EmptyState growth cannot be checked at all, which compounds Section 4's provenance finding rather than introducing a new one.

**Finding 8.6 — Do not recommend merging components based only on appearance; on inspection, the set's apparent duplicates are more often naming/classification drift than true redundancy.** Every pair examined above (Recommendation, Opportunity Cost/Summary, Decision History/Timeline Entry, Scenario Analysis/Comparison) describes components that are conceptually close but not identical once their actual field/behavior lists are compared — the problem is that three or four different documents each independently named and classified the same underlying concept, not that the product genuinely needs two components doing one job. The correct fix in every case is a single naming/classification decision, not a merge.

## 9. State and Lifecycle Audit

**Finding 9.1 — Internal, single-document count inconsistencies exist at both ends of the series, suggesting a systemic authoring-process issue rather than an isolated slip.**
*Severity:* Medium (documentation-integrity), but elevated to High in consequence because it recurs. *Evidence:* UX-012 §44 states *"The Fourteen Interaction Tokens"* while its own §30 table lists thirteen rows. UX-013E's own Canonical State Model lists 17 Lifecycle State rows in its detailed table but summarizes the same category as "(16)" at the document's end; its Validation States table lists 5 rows but is summarized as "(4)." *Consequence:* if this happened once, it would be a typo. Happening at the start (UX-012) and the end (UX-013E) of the series, in the same specific way (prose count vs. table count disagreeing), indicates the documents are generated or edited in a way that does not mechanically reconcile a stated total against its own enumerated list — a process risk for every future document in this series, not just these two.

**Finding 9.2 — UX-012 and UX-012B each present a differently-composed "thirteen states" list under the same document series.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* UX-012 §30's thirteen are interaction-token-flavored (hover, pressed, focused, selected, disabled, editing, expanded, collapsed, loading, saved, unsaved, updated, historical, acknowledged). UX-012B §13's thirteen are lifecycle/object-flavored (draft, saved, unsaved, under review, monitoring, completed, recorded, historical, updated, requires attention, deferred, superseded). These are not the same list, and no document notes that "thirteen" means two different things twelve pages apart in the same series.

**Finding 9.3 — Several of UX-013E's "canonical" states have no antecedent anywhere in the four documents this review could check them against (UX-012, UX-012B, UX-013A, UX-013B).**
*Severity:* High (compounds the Section 4/18 provenance problem directly). *Evidence, confirmed by direct search:* `breached`, `satisfied`, and `paused` (Monitoring Condition states) have no antecedent in UX-012's own Monitoring Condition lifecycle ("Established → Active → Approaching → Triggered → Acknowledged → Resolved"). `final` as a Decision lifecycle stage has no antecedent (UX-012's Final Decision Card has only two states, Draft/Recorded, not three). `outdated` and `partiallyAccepted` as formal AI-content *states* (as opposed to actions) have no antecedent in UX-012's Atlas Suggestion model. `historicalIntegrityViolation` and `recommendedCorrection` as Validation States have no antecedent in UX-012's validation section. UX-013E's own text attributes several of these directly to UX-013C ("`FinalDecision` (UX-013C)"), meaning this finding is a specific, concrete instance of Section 4's general provenance concern, not a separate issue.

**Finding 9.4 — "Superseded" carries two different mutability guarantees depending on which object it is applied to** (fully detailed as Finding 5.7). Restated here because it is a lifecycle-model conflict, not merely a naming one: a Decision that is Superseded is permanently immutable Historical Record (UX-012); reasoning content that is Superseded is explicitly "Read-only, not immutable" (UX-013B). An engineer implementing a generic `Superseded` state helper for both object types would build the wrong guarantee for one of them if they trust the word alone.

**Finding 9.5 — Real, but only partially specified, invalid-state-combination rules.** UX-013E states several explicit mutual-exclusion rules (`historical` + any editing-enabled state; `draft` + `recorded`; `accepted` + `rejected`) — a genuinely good practice, present only at the final-assembly stage. No earlier document (UX-012/012B) states any invalid combination at all for its own thirteen-state lists, meaning state combinability was entirely unspecified until the very last document in the chain. UX-013B's own cascade rule (a Broken Assumption automatically produces a Contradiction/Challenge) is a good, specific example of exactly this kind of rule done well — it should be the house style, not the exception.

**Finding 9.6 — "State" and "variant" are used inconsistently for the same underlying facts.** UX-012 calls the Draft/Recorded distinction on the Final Decision Card a "state" (§25); UX-013E models the identical fact as a `lifecycleState` **variant** property. UX-012B calls AI-generated-vs-user-modified a "state"; UX-013E's Canonical Property Model instead models authorship as **boolean properties**, and its own Canonical Variant Model explicitly flags this exact reclassification ("Historical | State + property... Historical is a lifecycle condition"). This is not wrong at the final layer — UX-013E's classification is more precise — but it means every earlier document's "state" vocabulary needs silent reinterpretation by anyone building from it directly rather than from UX-013E.

**Is UX-013E's canonical state model fully supported by the committed sources? No — stated plainly, per the task's explicit question.** As shown in Finding 9.3, a material portion of it is not derivable from UX-012/012B/013A/013B, and traces instead to the absent UX-013C/013D.

## 10. Authorship and AI Collaboration Audit

This is the audit dimension with the highest values-stakes in the whole set, because Atlas's own stated identity rests on a clean separation between what Atlas suggests and what the user decides (UX-012 glossary: *"AI Collaboration — ... Atlas suggests; the user decides."*). The core model is well-designed; the specific transition rules that operationalize it are not consistently specified.

**The model, as intended, is genuinely good.** UX-009A's three/four-tier ownership model (Atlas Generated–Read-Only / Atlas Suggested–Confirmable / User Owned / Locked) is clear, is repeated consistently in UX-010, and UX-013E's later property model (`isAtlasGenerated`, `isUserModified`, `authorship` enum) is a faithful, more implementable restatement of the same idea. UX-013E's own governing principle is exactly the right rule to state explicitly: *"AI authorship must never be silently cleared. The `authorship` prop and its rendered indicator persist through user edits unless the user explicitly performs a confirmation action that transfers authorship."*

**Finding 10.1 — Three separate documents each specify a different exact threshold for what counts as the "explicit user act" the governing principle above requires, and one of them contradicts the others outright.**
*Severity:* **Critical** — this bears directly on whether AI-originated content can appear as user-authored without a genuine user act, which the task explicitly asks this review to flag.
*Evidence:*
- UX-012 §46 (the strictest, two-step model): Accept alone labels content *"Atlas suggested / User accepted."* Only a **subsequent** user edit updates the label to *"User authored."*
- UX-013A (a looser standard): *"When `isAtlasGenerated` transitions to `isUserModified`... update the attribution silently. Do not prompt the user to confirm they have modified it."* Any edit, however trivial (a single punctuation fix), is sufficient — there is no meaningful-edit threshold.
- UX-012B (the loosest, and the one that actually contradicts UX-012): *"If accepted, the content copies into the user decision field and becomes user-modified-from-atlas state"* — triggered by **Accept alone**, with **zero subsequent edit required**, directly contradicting UX-012's own stricter two-step model for the identical event.

*Consequence:* depending on which of these three documents an implementer follows, the exact same user action (clicking "Accept" and doing nothing else) either leaves content labeled "Atlas suggested" (UX-012) or relabels it "user-modified" (UX-012B) — two materially different provenance claims about the same database row, both textually supported by a "governing" document in this set. Given that the product's own stated value proposition depends on this distinction being trustworthy, this is not a documentation nitpick; it is a place where the specification itself would let a future engineer build a feature that silently misattributes Atlas-originated content to the user, in direct violation of UX-013E's own stated governing principle. *Recommendation:* this needs one explicit, dated product decision (not a silent pick by whichever engineer implements it first) — see Section 21, Finding C-2, and Section 23.

**Finding 10.2 — Persistence rules are, in every case checked, precise and well-stated.** UX-012B: *"Suggestions are not preserved in the decision record. Only the final content of the field... is recorded."* UX-013E's Action Inventory distinguishes exactly which actions are preserved, not preserved, or logged-but-hidden per action (Accept/Dismiss/Reject each behave differently). This is a genuine strength and should not be lost when the authorship-threshold question above is resolved.

**Finding 10.3 — Visual consistency for authorship indication is thorough at the final-assembly layer.** UX-013E's seven-category authorship table (User-authored / Atlas-generated / User-confirmed AI / User-edited AI / System-generated / Mixed / Unknown), each with a distinct label and indicator rule, is the most complete authorship taxonomy in the set and is a good foundation — once Finding 10.1 is resolved, this table is the right place to encode the resolution.

## 11. Historical Integrity Audit

**The domain rule is stated with total consistency and real conviction across every document that touches it:** *"History is permanent. No recorded content is ever modified, overwritten, or deleted"* (UX-012, restated near-verbatim at least three times across UX-012 and UX-012C). Amendments and supersessions are consistently modeled as **additive**, never in-place, both for Decisions (UX-012's Decision Amendment: *"amendments do not modify the original Decision. They are additive historical records"*) and for Outcomes (UX-013E: *"Do not retroactively rewrite the original decision; Outcome is additive"*). This is a well-designed, consistently-applied domain rule and is one of the set's clearest strengths.

**Finding 11.1 — "Historical component" (UX-012) vs. "historical state on a live component" (UX-013E) is a real reclassification, but it is disclosed, not silent, and is therefore lower-severity than it first appears.**
*Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* UX-012 §27 defines Historical Decision, Historical Review, Historical Assumption, and Historical Timeline Entry as their own named components. UX-013E explicitly reverses this and says so directly: *"Historical Decision is not a separate component. It is `DecisionCard` with `isHistorical={true}`... No component named `HistoricalDecision` exists in the canonical library."* *Which rule is domain vs. presentation:* the underlying domain rule (immutability, permanent labeling) is identical in both documents; only the *component-identity* classification changed. This is a presentation/architecture-layer correction, not a product-rule contradiction, and UX-013E does the reader the courtesy of stating the change explicitly rather than silently reusing the old name for something new.

**Finding 11.2 — A wording-level tension in UX-009A between "locked after recording" and "versioned after post-recording changes," resolved correctly but not explicitly by later documents.**
*Severity:* Low-Medium. *Evidence:* UX-009A states both *"All fields... lock after Record Decision is selected"* and, a few lines later, *"Any changes to monitoring conditions or invalidation conditions after recording create a visible amendment entry."* Read in isolation these sound contradictory; UX-012's Decision Amendment definition ("amendments do not modify the original... additive historical records") resolves the apparent tension correctly, but UX-009A itself never states the resolving principle, leaving a reader of UX-009A alone with an apparent contradiction that only a later document clears up.

**Finding 11.3 — "Deletion never happens" vs. "components can be Deprecated/Removed" is a genuine terminology-overlap risk, though the documents are careful to keep the underlying rules separate.** UX-013E is explicit that a *design-system component's* engineering export can be "Removed" after a deprecation window, while the Figma artifact is "archived (not deleted) — historical references may still link to it." This is correctly scoped to **system/governance** artifacts, not **user decision data**, and the documents do not actually conflate the two — but the shared vocabulary ("removed," "retired") sitting next to an absolute "never deleted" product rule is worth a documentation clarification, not because the rule is wrong, but because it is easy to misquote out of context.

**Domain rule vs. presentation rule, stated plainly for this section as a whole:** every genuine domain rule found in this audit (immutability, additive amendment, no deletion of decision content) is stated consistently everywhere it appears. Every tension found is at the presentation/component-identity layer (how the immutable fact is displayed or named), never at the level of what is actually guaranteed to the user's data. This is a meaningfully positive finding on its own terms.

## 12. Navigation and Context Preservation Audit

**Finding 12.1 — The stated maximum-overlay-depth rule is not reconciled against several nested affordances the documents also specify, and the rule's own author acknowledges it is unvalidated.**
*Severity:* Medium-High. *Evidence:* UX-012C states plainly: *"Atlas supports a maximum of two overlapping Workspace layers. If a third overlay is requested, it replaces the second layer rather than adding a third."* But UX-009A separately specifies at least three affordances reachable from within an already-open Decision Workspace that each open additional surfaces — "View full analysis" (a "secondary context surface — a slide-in panel or nested overlay"), "View prior decisions" (opens Atlas Memory), and "Compare with recorded" (a side-by-side view) — without stating whether any of them count as the second layer, a third that would evict the second, or a different, uncapped mechanism entirely. UX-012C itself partially hedges this with a conditional branch for Historical Record access (sometimes an in-place content replace, sometimes "a separate overlay," depending on whether the record is for the same or a different subject), which is exactly the kind of case-by-case complexity that makes a clean two-layer rule hard to apply consistently. *Self-acknowledged in the text itself:* UX-012C's own "Remaining Interaction Questions" states, *"This rule may need to be reconsidered if user research reveals workflows that require deeper navigation chains. The two-layer rule is a design judgment, not a fundamental constraint"* — a genuinely honest disclosure, but one that leaves the actual implementation question open. *Consequence:* engineers will need to make a case-by-case judgment call for each of these affordances that the documentation does not currently make for them.

**Finding 12.2 — The Dialog-vs-wide-Overlay boundary is explicitly flagged by the documents themselves as unresolved, with a stated consequence.** UX-013A: *"the boundary between these two types — specifically when to use a Dialog versus a wide Overlay for contextual information — requires a decision rule that is currently stated only as a principle... If the boundary is not clear, designers and engineers will inconsistently choose between Dialog and Overlay, creating interaction inconsistency across Workspaces."* This is the set at its most self-aware — the document names the exact risk this review would otherwise have to surface independently.

**Finding 12.3 — Return/back and scroll-restoration behavior are specified well, with one genuinely unresolved boundary carried across two document generations without ever being closed.** The Return Navigation model itself ("preserves context on return — scroll position, draft, expanded state") is clear and consistent. But the definition of "session" that scroll restoration depends on is flagged as open in UX-013A ("whether a session ends when the browser closes, when the user logs out, or after a defined period of inactivity has not been specified") and is **still** listed as an open architectural question in UX-013E, the very last document in the chain — meaning two full component-specification generations passed without this ever being settled. *Severity:* Medium — not blocking, since a reasonable default (browser-session-based) could be assumed for a first implementation, but its persistence across two generations is a process signal worth noting for Section 19.

**Browser-history behavior is specified with unusual precision and is a genuine strength**: *"Workspace overlay navigation is not reflected in browser history... The user's browser back button returns them to the prior Atlas surface, not to a prior Workspace overlay state"* (UX-012C) — a clear, implementable, testable rule with no contradiction found anywhere else in the set.

## 13. Responsive Architecture Audit

**Finding 13.1 — A direct tension between a stated design value ("no functional content hidden on mobile") and multiple concrete specifications that reduce content on mobile.**
*Severity:* Medium-High. *Evidence:* UX-011 states, twice, in absolute terms: *"No functional content is hidden or moved on tablet... The adaptation is purely spatial"* and *"Mobile must not feel like a degraded version of desktop... The experience is simpler in scope but not lesser in quality."* Yet UX-012C states mobile *"collapse defaults more aggressively,"* UX-013A states the Workspace Toolbar *"collapses entirely"* on mobile with actions demoted to an overflow menu, UX-013E's condensation table demotes SectionHeader actions to "maximum 1 visible on mobile," and UX-013B's Scenario Analysis explicitly allows Conditions/Implications to be collapsed by default on mobile "to reduce initial screen length." *Consequence:* "purely spatial, nothing hidden" and "collapses entirely" cannot both be literally true of the same Toolbar component; an implementer has textual grounds to justify either a fully-parity or a reduced-functionality mobile Toolbar, and the documents do not state which reading should win.

**Finding 13.2 — The Comparison component's responsive model requires a genuinely nontrivial engineering pattern that the documents do not acknowledge as a cost.** *Severity:* Medium. *Evidence:* UX-013B specifies real `<table>`/`<th scope="col">`/`<th scope="row">` markup at desktop/tablet widths, then explicitly states *"no table structure (tables do not stack semantically)"* at mobile widths. Maintaining one accessible component that is genuinely tabular at wide viewports and genuinely non-tabular at narrow ones typically requires two distinct render paths keyed to breakpoint, not pure CSS reflow — a real, non-trivial build cost the specification does not flag as one.

**Finding 13.3 — At least one hover-dependent micro-interaction has no stated concrete touch equivalent, despite a general rule that all hover interactions must have one.** UX-011 describes the Atlas Suggestion affordance's resting-state visibility as hover-revealed ("it may be partially visible or dimmed in its resting state... On hover, the affordance label becomes fully visible"). UX-012C's general rule ("hover equivalents are achieved through persistent controls, tap-to-reveal, or long-press") is never applied concretely to this specific component. This is conceptually attractive (a quiet, unobtrusive offer that reveals itself) and is exactly the kind of interaction likely to be implemented inconsistently on touch devices absent a concrete rule.

**Finding 13.4 — One unexplained deviation from the otherwise-universal 44×44px touch-target minimum.** UX-010 states the Record Decision button specifically gets a 48px height minimum on mobile, larger than the 44×44px rule stated everywhere else in the set, with no explanation given for the increase. Likely intentional (it is the single most consequential button in the product), but undocumented as a deliberate exception, which risks being "corrected" back to 44px by an engineer unaware it was a deliberate choice.

## 14. Accessibility Architecture Audit

**Finding 14.1 — Native `disabled` semantics are implicitly assumed by early documents in a way that would violate a requirement stated by a slightly later document, and the conflict is only resolved by even later, engineering-facing documents.**
*Severity:* **Critical**, for the specific reason the task asks to flag: a documented conflict with standard HTML/ARIA behavior.
*Evidence:* UX-009A and UX-011 describe the disabled Record Decision button in plain terms ("Disabled state: reduced emphasis, cursor: not-allowed") with no qualification of *how* disabled is implemented. UX-010, in the same document family, separately *requires* that button's adjacent explanation to be reachable and to announce on focus/state change: *"Completion gate explanations (the text adjacent to the disabled Record Decision button) are announced when the button is focused and when its state changes."* **The standard HTML `disabled` attribute removes an element from the tab order entirely and prevents it from ever receiving focus** — so if UX-009A/010/011's plain "disabled" language were implemented literally using the native attribute, UX-010's own focus-announcement requirement would be structurally impossible to satisfy. Only UX-013A and UX-012C, written later, resolve this correctly and explicitly: *"they carry `aria-disabled='true'` (not the HTML `disabled` attribute, which removes them from tab order)... primary actions in disabled state remain in the tab order."* *Consequence:* an implementer working from UX-009A/010/011 alone — plausible, since those are the documents that specify the Record Decision button's actual behavior in the Decision Workspace — has no textual instruction to avoid the native `disabled` attribute, and would need to already know ARIA best practice independently of this documentation set to avoid the conflict. The correction exists in the set, but not in the same document that states the requirement it's needed to satisfy.

**Finding 14.2 — A direct, unreconciled quantitative contradiction on zoom/reflow support.**
*Severity:* Medium-High. *Evidence:* UX-013A and UX-013B both set the requirement at *"200% to 200%"* ("At 200% zoom: no content is clipped, no horizontal scroll"). UX-013E — the document that claims cross-cutting accessibility governance authority — states *"All content reflows at 200% and 400% browser zoom... No content clipped at 400%,"* introducing a stricter ceiling with no note that this is a new addition over the two earlier component-spec documents. *Consequence:* not functionally contradictory in the sense that 400%-compliant markup will also satisfy a 200% requirement, but it is a governance ambiguity — no document states which figure is officially required for compliance sign-off, and a QA process checking against UX-013A/B alone would pass content that UX-013E's own stricter bar would fail.

**Everything else checked is well-specified and internally consistent:** landmark regions (a complete, consistent `<main>`/`<header>`/`<footer>`/`<nav>`/`role="dialog"` mapping repeated verbatim across UX-013A and UX-013E), heading hierarchy (one `<h1>` per Workspace, no skipped levels, stated identically in both documents), focus movement and restoration on Dialog/overlay open-close (clear, testable, and consistently the "absolute governing rule" per UX-012C), non-color status communication (stated as an absolute rule in three separate documents with identical wording), and reduced-motion behavior (one deliberate, explicitly-disclosed exception for a single 400ms pause, which is exactly how an exception to a blanket rule should be documented). These are all genuine strengths and should be treated as the house standard for any future accessibility specification in this series.

## 15. Token and Visual-System Audit

**Finding 15.1 — UX-012D's own concrete token vocabulary is never actually used by the two component-specification volumes that came after it, despite both nominally following its naming convention.**
*Severity:* **High.** *Evidence:* UX-012 states the naming convention once (`category.role.variant`, e.g. `conclusion.text.primary`). UX-012D's own worked semantic-token examples use names like `space.inter-section`, `color.text.primary`, `color.border.contradiction.unresolved`, `type.conclusion`. **None of these exact strings appear anywhere in UX-013A or UX-013B.** Instead, UX-013A uses `surface.workspace.background`, `text.heading.primary`, `spacing.level1`–`level6`, `status.draft.text`; UX-013B uses `reasoning.factor.name`, `border.factor.weakening`, `border.challenge.blocking`, `text.attribution.atlas`. Both families follow the stated *pattern* (dot-hierarchy, semantic role naming) but share **zero concrete token identity** with UX-012D or with each other's naming instincts for equivalent concepts (`spacing.levelN` vs. `space.inter-section`; `text.heading.primary` vs. `color.text.primary`). *Consequence:* if implemented literally as three separate token files, Figma and engineering would end up maintaining two (or three) non-interoperable token vocabularies that all claim to be "the Atlas token system," each internally consistent but incompatible with the others at the level that actually matters for a shared component library — the literal string used in a stylesheet or a Figma variable binding.

**Finding 15.2 — UX-013E's claimed "pure extension, no deprecation" of the token system is asserted at the category level, not verified at the token-identity level.** UX-013E states: *"No existing tokens identified for deprecation. The Atlas token vocabulary established in UX-012 is extended by the above additions; none of the UX-012 tokens are removed."* Given Finding 15.1, this claim is not false, but it is incomplete: UX-013A/013B never actually adopted UX-012D's concrete tokens in the first place, so there is very little concrete UX-012D vocabulary left to preserve — "extension" is true of the category *scheme*, not of a shared, populated token dictionary.

**Finding 15.3 — Is UX-012 the correct token authority?** Yes, at the level of *category taxonomy and naming convention* (fifteen categories, the `category.role.variant` pattern, the twelve prohibited naming styles) — this part of UX-012D is thorough and well-reasoned, and nothing downstream contradicts the *rules*, only the concrete *instances*. UX-012 is the wrong place to look for a concrete, implementation-ready token dictionary, because one was never actually carried through consistently past UX-012D itself.

**Finding 15.4 — New token groups UX-013E introduces (authorship.*, decision.state.*, monitoring.state.*, ai.state.*, confidence.level.*) are reasonable and well-named, but inherit the state-provenance problem from Section 9/18** where their underlying state names (e.g. `monitoring.state.breached`) trace to the unverifiable UX-013C.

## 16. Figma Architecture Audit

**Finding 16.1 — The proposed Figma architecture is unusually complete and concrete for a document set at this stage, and is a genuine strength.** UX-013E specifies a full library page tree (Foundation, Metadata & Provenance, Reasoning, Decision, Monitoring, Historical, AI Collaboration, Feedback, Loading & Availability, Overlay & Dialog, Notification, Patterns, Workspace Templates), an explicit component-set-property model (variants as component-set properties; non-user-selectable states as boolean properties, never variant enum values), a "Protected Properties" list preventing consuming designers from overriding token bindings or historical treatment, and a concrete Definition-of-Done checklist per component (nine explicit gates including accessibility annotation and Design-System-owner review). This is production-grade Figma governance thinking, not aspirational language.

**Finding 16.2 — The proposed total component count (87 canonical components, plus 18 Actions, 13 Behaviors, 20 Composed Patterns, 21 Semantic Concepts, 4 Templates) is large but the variant-cap discipline (48 max per component set, explicit decomposition rule if exceeded) is the correct mitigation, and is stated, not merely implied.** *Severity:* Low, given the mitigation is already in the text — flagged here only because the total figure itself rests partly on the unverifiable UX-013C/013D component counts (Section 18), so the true final count cannot be confirmed independent of that gap.

**Finding 16.3 — Markdown, not Figma, is explicitly and correctly stated as the source of truth.** *"The design specification produced by UX-012A through UX-012D is the authoritative source for component behavior, states, and semantic meaning... Figma represents components visually"* (UX-012D/UX-013E). This review does not need to caution against treating Figma as authoritative — the documents already establish the correct relationship themselves.

**Finding 16.4 — Naming discipline for Figma is genuinely good.** A specific, non-generic naming rule ("`Conclusion`, `SupportingFactors`" preferred; "`Panel`, `Card`, `Modal`, `Box`, `Wrapper`, `Widget`" prohibited, each mapped to a canonical replacement) removes a very common source of Figma-library decay (generic, non-searchable names). This should be preserved unchanged.

## 17. Engineering Architecture Audit

**Finding 17.1 — A direct, stated self-contradiction about the design system's own scope boundary regarding framework choice.**
*Severity:* High. *Evidence:* UX-012D explicitly disclaims authority over this exact question: *"Engineering implementation details (choice of framework, component API design, state management approach) are engineering decisions — they are not specified by UX-012."* UX-013E's entire "Engineering Component API Standard" and "Shared Behavior Architecture" sections are nonetheless written exclusively in React/TypeScript-specific vocabulary throughout — typed interfaces returning `React.ReactNode`, hook names following React's `use*` convention, named concrete hooks (`useFocusManagement`, `useUndoWindow(5000)`, `useLoadingDelay(300)`, `useScrollRestoration`), and the `Behavior` classification itself is defined as "a React hook, utility function, or service module." *Consequence:* the document that claims not to dictate framework has, in practice, fully committed the component library's public API shape to React — which may well be the right engineering choice, but it is a decision UX-012D explicitly said belonged to engineering, made silently inside a document whose own charter says it wouldn't make it.

**Finding 17.2 — Package/module boundaries and dependency direction are specified with real rigor and are a genuine strength.** UX-013E's eleven-layer package model (`@atlas/tokens` → `@atlas/primitives` → `@atlas/foundation` → ... → `@atlas/decision` → `@atlas/monitoring`) with an explicit "No Circular Dependencies Found" self-check, and the specific, correctly-architected rule that "Reasoning components reference Decision components via typed IDs... they do not import Decision component implementations," is exactly the right pattern for a component library expected to grow across many Workspaces. This should be preserved and enforced mechanically (e.g., via lint rules) once implementation begins.

**Finding 17.3 — Complete absence of any SSR/hydration guidance anywhere across all nine engineering-adjacent documents, despite otherwise granular client-side specificity.** *Severity:* Medium — not blocking for Foundation-tier work, but a real gap the documents otherwise would be expected to cover given how specific they are about scroll restoration timing, autosave intervals, and focus management. A confirmed full-text search across the reviewed engineering-facing documents found zero relevant mentions.

**Finding 17.4 — Several passages prescribe implementation techniques specific enough to constrain ordinary frontend flexibility, beyond what a design specification typically needs to state.** Examples: *"Do not use `position: fixed` for Header or Footer — use sticky within a defined scroll container"*; *"Avoid animating `height` from 0 to `auto` with CSS alone — use JS-measured heights or the Web Animations API"*; a specific recommendation to use `react-virtual` for Decision History virtualization. None of these are wrong, and several reflect real, hard-won frontend lessons — but stating them as specification rather than implementation guidance blurs the same design/engineering boundary UX-012D explicitly tried to protect (Finding 17.1), and should be reframed as non-binding engineering notes rather than governed requirements.

**Finding 17.5 — Testing expectations are specified with unusually good tiering (leaf component / composite / composed pattern each get progressively more test-coverage requirements), and this is a genuine strength worth preserving as-is.**

## 18. Missing-Source Impact Analysis

### UX-006 — Portfolio Workspace Philosophy

**What cannot be verified without it:** Whether the Portfolio Workspace's stated purpose, entry/exit conditions, non-duplication boundary against the Dashboard and Investment Workspace, and Conclusion/Decision ownership model in UX-007A/007P actually reflect an approved philosophy, or are simply the wireframe/polish authors' own working assumptions carried forward without a governing source. UX-007A and UX-007P both explicitly cite UX-006 as their philosophical foundation and neither attempts to restate it. *Evidence impact:* High — this is not a case of the philosophy being "implied" elsewhere; it is cited by name and title as an authority the present documents assume without restating. *Implementation impact:* Medium — the wireframe/polish content is specific enough to build from directly, so day-one implementation is not blocked, but any future change to Portfolio Workspace's scope or purpose has no governing document to check against. *Governance impact:* High — there is currently no way to adjudicate a future dispute about "is this within Portfolio Workspace's intended scope" the way UX-004 lets such a dispute be adjudicated for Investment Workspace. *Reconstruction priority:* **Recommended, not merely for numbering completeness** — Portfolio Workspace is one of four core Workspaces and is the only one whose governing philosophy is entirely absent from the repository; this is a real, not cosmetic, gap.

### UX-007 — Portfolio Workspace Screen Specification

**What cannot be verified without it:** The intended information architecture, reading order, and section-level interaction-ownership rules for the Portfolio Workspace at the specification level the Decision Workspace enjoys via UX-009. UX-007A's own 40-section wireframe implies a 14-item content order (Conclusion → What Changed → Portfolio Health → ... → Final Decision State), but states explicitly that this is a *refinement* of an existing screen specification, not a first statement of one: *"UX-007A should refine and formalize this implementation rather than replace its central interaction model."* *Evidence impact:* High — the wireframe's own section order can be read back as a de facto screen spec, but this review cannot confirm it matches an approved UX-007 rather than reflecting wireframe-stage drift from one. *Implementation impact:* Low-Medium — sufficient structural detail exists in UX-007A/007P to build from directly. *Governance impact:* Medium-High, for the same reason as UX-006. *Reconstruction priority:* Recommended if Portfolio Workspace is scheduled for near-term implementation; not urgent if Portfolio Workspace work is not imminent, since the wireframe/polish pair is functionally sufficient to build the current, already-approved implementation.

### UX-013C — Decision & Monitoring Components

**Which Decision/Monitoring component claims in UX-013E cannot be traced to an available completed source:** Nearly all of the granular ones. Confirmed independently derivable from UX-012 alone: the *existence and one-paragraph purpose* of Decision History, Decision Amendment, Monitoring Condition, Review Trigger, Invalidation Trigger, Historical Decision/Review. **Not derivable from anything else in the committed set:** the ~27-component count, all seven DecisionCard lifecycle-variant names, every specific prop (`monitoringConditionId`, `lifecycleState`), the `breached`/`satisfied`/`paused` Monitoring states (Finding 9.3), and every specific UX-013E "supersedes" claim about UX-013C's own naming decisions. *Evidence impact:* Critical for the granular claims, none for the broad-concept claims. *Implementation impact:* High — this is the tier (Decision, Monitoring) that governs the single most safety-critical Workspace action (recording and later amending a permanent decision); building it from UX-013E's account alone means building from a source this review cannot confirm is accurate. *Governance impact:* High — UX-013E's own supersession claim over UX-013C cannot be checked, since the thing being superseded is absent. *Reconstruction priority:* **High, and not merely for numbering completeness** — this is the one missing-document gap this review actively recommends closing before Decision/Monitoring-tier implementation proceeds, because the alternative is building the product's core commitment mechanism from an unverifiable secondhand account.

### UX-013D — AI Collaboration, Metadata & System Components

**Which AI Collaboration/Metadata/System claims in UX-013E cannot be traced to an available completed source:** The same pattern as UX-013C. Broadly corroborated: Atlas Suggestion, Atlas Insight, Atlas Warning exist as named concepts with one-paragraph definitions in UX-012. **Not corroborated:** the ~35-component count, `MetadataBlock`, `SourceReference`, `StatusPresentation`, `SkeletonState`, `AIWorkingState`, `AtlasRecommendationPresentation`, the specific merge decisions UX-013E attributes to this volume, and the `outdated`/`partiallyAccepted` AI-content states (Finding 9.3). *Evidence impact:* Critical for the granular claims. *Implementation impact:* High, given Finding 10.1's authorship-threshold contradiction sits exactly in this component family's territory — meaning the tier with the least verifiable provenance is also the tier with the most values-critical, currently-contradictory rule in the whole set. *Governance impact:* High, same reasoning as UX-013C. *Reconstruction priority:* **High** — for the same reason as UX-013C, and more urgently given Finding 10.1.

**A general note on reconstruction priority, applying to all four:** this review does not recommend reconstructing any of the four missing documents merely to fill a numbering gap. UX-006/007 are recommended for reconstruction because Portfolio Workspace implementation cannot otherwise be governed the way the other three Workspaces are. UX-013C/013D are recommended for reconstruction (or, at minimum, independent re-derivation and re-verification of UX-013E's specific claims about them) because the alternative is implementing the product's most safety-critical component family from a source that cannot currently be checked against anything.

## 19. Scalability and Maintainability

**Conceptual scalability.** The core reasoning shape (Conclusion → Supporting Factors → Challenges → Decision) is well-articulated and durable — it should transfer cleanly to most future analytical Workspaces. The place it will strain first, as noted in Section 6, is a genuinely different future Workspace (Scenario, Research) whose primary output is not itself a Decision; nothing in the set discusses how the Reasoning component family or the six-level hierarchy would need to flex for that case, and Section 7's unresolved hierarchy conflict makes this harder to reason about, not easier.

**Component scalability.** UX-013E's layered package architecture (Section 17.2) is genuinely well-suited to growth — new Workspaces can add a new top-layer package without touching Foundation/Reasoning. The state-explosion risk (Section 8.5) is self-monitored with an explicit cap, which is the correct posture. The classification-model churn (two types in UX-012 → ten types in UX-013E, Finding 5.10/8) is a one-time cost that has already been paid; it should not recur if UX-013E's taxonomy is genuinely adopted going forward rather than revised again at the next "final assembly."

**Documentation scalability.** This is the area most likely to fail first, and the evidence is already visible within this one document set: two separate "final assembly" efforts (UX-012E→UX-012, UX-013A–D→UX-013E) each produced their own from-scratch classification/reconciliation process, with different naming outcomes (Finding 3.2) and, in the 013 case, unverifiable dependence on component-family volumes that either were never finished or were never captured (Section 18). If a third design-system generation (say, a Monitoring-and-Review system, or a Portfolio-tier component library) follows the same "write four volumes, then reconcile in a fifth" pattern without a stronger process guarantee that every volume actually gets captured and cross-checked before the reconciling document is written, the UX-013C/013D gap will recur, and at a larger scale each time, since each generation's component count is larger than the last (UX-012's inventory was on the order of tens of components; UX-013E's is 87 canonical components plus dozens of non-component items).

**Governance scalability.** UX-012D's governance apparatus (versioning, deprecation windows, consistency-audit method, thirteen named anti-patterns) is a genuinely mature foundation. Its weakest point is exactly the one this review keeps returning to: a governance system is only as trustworthy as its own self-audits, and this review found at least one self-audit claim (Finding 8.2, UX-013A's "no conflicts" claim) that does not hold up, and at least one internal count inconsistency at both ends of the series (Finding 9.1). A governance model this thorough deserves an actual, independent verification step before each "supersedes" declaration is trusted, not merely a self-declared audit section within the same document making the claim.

**API stability.** UX-013E's component APIs are well-typed and reasonably stable in shape, but Finding 17.1 (silent React-framework commitment) means "API stability" currently also means "commitment to a specific framework's idioms," which is a larger and less reversible commitment than the documents' own stated design-system charter intends.

**Onboarding burden.** A new designer or engineer joining this project faces a genuinely large document set (22,000+ lines) with at least the contradictions catalogued in this review still live. The strongest mitigation already present in the documents themselves is the "Remaining Questions" section nearly every document includes — this is a good practice and should be preserved, but it currently functions as a list of acknowledged gaps rather than a tracked, resolved backlog (several of the same questions, e.g. the scroll-restoration session boundary, appear unresolved across two document generations, per Finding 12.3).

**Most likely sources of design debt, ranked:** (1) the information-hierarchy conflict (Section 7), because it affects every future Reasoning-tier component's visual priority; (2) the authorship-threshold contradiction (Finding 10.1), because it affects data provenance, which is expensive to retrofit once real user data exists; (3) the token-identity mismatch (Finding 15.1), because it means the "shared" design system currently has no shared concrete vocabulary; (4) the unverifiable UX-013C/013D dependency (Section 18), because it means a large fraction of the Decision/Monitoring/AI component families cannot be trusted without independent re-derivation, and that re-derivation cost only grows the longer implementation proceeds on the current, unverified basis.

## 22. Top Ten Recommendations

Ranked by long-term architectural value, not ease of execution. "Source correction" = fix within an existing document. "New governing decision" = a decision no existing document has made, requiring explicit authorship. "Implementation evidence" = best resolved by building and observing, not by more specification. "User research" = needs data from real users, not just internal reasoning.

1. **Resolve the six-level information hierarchy conflict (C-1) as one new governing decision, amending UX-012 directly.** Why it matters: this is the single highest-leverage fix in the set — it affects the visual treatment of every Reasoning component in every Workspace. Documents affected: UX-012, UX-012A (retire or explicitly mark historical). Timing: before implementation.

2. **Resolve the authorship-transfer threshold contradiction (C-2) as a new governing decision, ratified into UX-013E's authorship table.** Why it matters: this is the one place the documented specification currently permits a values-violating outcome (silent AI-to-user authorship transfer) the product's own principles forbid. Documents affected: UX-012, UX-012B, UX-013A, UX-013E. Timing: before implementation.

3. **Resolve the Decision Workspace canonical section order (C-3) via source correction, reconciling UX-012's compressed sequence against UX-009A/010/011's mutually-consistent 13-section order.** Why it matters: this is the core information architecture of the product's most safety-critical screen. Documents affected: UX-012 (correction), UX-013B (correction). Timing: before implementation.

4. **Resolve the completion-gate field-count contradiction (C-4) as a new governing decision.** Why it matters: gates the single most irreversible user action in the product. Documents affected: UX-009A, UX-012, UX-012C. Timing: before implementation.

5. **Independently re-derive or reconstruct UX-013C and UX-013D's genuinely load-bearing claims (C-5), rather than continuing to build Decision/Monitoring/AI-tier components from UX-013E's unverifiable secondhand account.** Why it matters: roughly half the canonical component library's most safety-critical tiers currently rest on nothing this repository can check. Documents affected: new UX-013C/013D (or a documented, explicit re-verification pass on UX-013E's relevant sections). Timing: before implementation of Decision, Monitoring, and AI Collaboration tiers specifically (Foundation and Reasoning tiers are not blocked).

6. **Fix the disabled-control ARIA conflict (C-6) by adding the `aria-disabled`-not-`disabled` instruction directly into UX-009A, UX-010, and UX-011, not only into the later engineering documents.** Why it matters: the documents that actually specify this button's behavior currently license an accessibility bug. Documents affected: UX-009A, UX-010, UX-011 (source correction, pulling forward existing UX-013A/012C language). Timing: before implementation.

7. **Produce a single, concrete, shared token dictionary and require both UX-013A and UX-013B's next revision to reference it by exact string, closing the H-4 token-identity gap.** Why it matters: a "shared design system" whose two component-spec volumes use zero overlapping concrete token names is not actually shared yet. Documents affected: UX-012D, UX-013A, UX-013B. Timing: before implementation, or concurrently with first implementation if timeline pressure requires it (this is more tractable than 1–6).

8. **Ratify the React/TypeScript commitment (H-5) as an explicit engineering decision, either by revising UX-012D's disclaimer or by adding a companion engineering-charter document that owns this choice openly.** Why it matters: closes a real, if minor, integrity gap between what the design system claims not to decide and what it has in fact already decided. Documents affected: UX-012D, UX-013E. Timing: during first implementation (low urgency, easy to fix whenever noticed).

9. **Reconcile the maximum-overlay-depth rule (H-6) against the specific nested affordances (View full analysis, View prior decisions, Compare with recorded) that the current two-layer rule does not address — ideally informed by early implementation evidence, since UX-012C itself calls the two-layer rule "a design judgment, not a fundamental constraint."** Why it matters: real navigation-complexity risk, self-acknowledged by the documents. Documents affected: UX-012C, UX-009A. Timing: after implementation evidence — this is a case where building a first version and observing real nesting patterns will produce a better rule than more upfront specification would.

10. **Resolve the "nothing hidden on mobile" vs. "collapses entirely" tension (H-8) with a single explicit rule per affected component (Toolbar, SectionHeader actions, Scenario Analysis), rather than leaving both an absolute value statement and a contradicting concrete rule in the set.** Why it matters: affects mobile parity commitments the product may be making to users without realizing it. Documents affected: UX-011, UX-012C, UX-013A, UX-013B. Timing: during first implementation, informed by real mobile screen-space constraints.

No stylistic recommendation is included in this list, per the task's instruction; several genuine documentation-quality issues (Section 21 Low findings) are real but are explicitly excluded here as non-structural.

## 23. Mandatory Before Implementation

Conservative, by the task's own instruction — this list is deliberately short and contains only items that would produce an unresolved contradiction in the shipped product if skipped, not general imperfections:

1. **C-1 — the information-hierarchy conflict** must be resolved before any Reasoning-tier component's visual-priority implementation begins, because the two candidate answers point in opposite directions for the same content.
2. **C-2 — the authorship-transfer threshold** must be resolved before any component that displays or persists AI-vs-user authorship is built, because the current specification would let two conformant implementations produce opposite, values-violating outcomes for the identical user action.
3. **C-3 — the Decision Workspace section order** must be resolved before the Decision Workspace's own layout is implemented, because there is currently no single order to implement.
4. **C-4 — the completion-gate field count** must be resolved before the Record Decision validation logic is written, because it gates an irreversible action and the specification currently states two different, incompatible thresholds.
5. **C-6 — the disabled-control ARIA instruction** must be added to the Decision Workspace-specific documents before the Record Decision button is implemented, because the alternative is a specific, avoidable, and already-identified accessibility defect.

**C-5 (the UX-013C/013D provenance gap) is mandatory before Decision-, Monitoring-, and AI-Collaboration-tier implementation specifically, but is not mandatory before Foundation-tier (UX-013A) or Reasoning-tier (UX-013B) implementation begins**, since those two tiers' provenance is independently checkable and largely sound. This is stated as a scoped, not blanket, blocker, consistent with the instruction to be conservative about what truly blocks initial work.

Everything else in Section 21 (all High, Medium, and Low findings not listed above) is explicitly **not** classified as mandatory-before-implementation — see Section 24.

## 24. Safe to Defer

- **H-1, H-2, H-3, M-1 through M-4 (naming/classification conflicts not tied to a gating condition or an irreversible action)** — safe to defer to implementation evidence, because building the Reasoning components as currently (ambiguously) specified will not produce a broken product; it will, at worst, produce a component named or classified one of two reasonable ways, correctable in a later normalization pass once real usage clarifies which naming reads better in practice.
- **H-4 (token-identity mismatch)** — safe to defer to the start of implementation rather than requiring it fully resolved beforehand, since Foundation-tier work can proceed on its own self-consistent token set in the interim; deferral is safe as long as it is tracked, not silently accepted.
- **H-6 (overlay-depth rule)** — explicitly safe, and better, to defer to implementation evidence: UX-012C's own text states this is "a design judgment, not a fundamental constraint," and real usage patterns from an initial build will produce better information than further upfront reasoning.
- **H-7 (zoom 200%/400% discrepancy)** — safe to defer to accessibility testing, which will empirically determine whether 200%-only markup actually satisfies real assistive-technology users; the stricter 400% bar can be adopted after that evidence exists rather than assumed necessary now.
- **H-8, M-9 (mobile collapse tension, Comparison responsive markup)** — safe to defer to implementation evidence and, ideally, usability testing on real mobile devices, since the correct amount of mobile content-reduction is fundamentally an empirical ergonomics question, not one more specification can resolve in the abstract.
- **M-5 (internal count inconsistencies), M-8 (state/variant classification drift), L-1 through L-8 (terminology/naming/documentation-hygiene findings)** — all safe to defer to a dedicated documentation-normalization pass after implementation begins; none of these affects what gets built, only how consistently it is described.
- **M-10 (scroll-restoration session boundary), M-11 (SSR/hydration silence)** — safe to defer to implementation evidence; a reasonable default (browser-tab-scoped session; client-rendered-only for a first version) can be adopted now and revisited once real deployment requirements (or a genuine SSR need) materialize.
- **M-12 (overly specific implementation directives)** — safe to defer; treat as non-binding suggestions during implementation rather than resolving them as a precondition.

Deferral is judged safe in every case above specifically because none of these items can, on its own, produce a contradiction that reaches the user as an inconsistent or unsafe product experience the way the five Mandatory items (Section 23) could.

## 25. Overall Assessment

**Major strengths:** an unusually disciplined document-layering model (Section 3) that keeps philosophy, screen architecture, wireframe, visual design, and design system cleanly separated; a genuinely strong and consistently-applied historical-immutability domain rule (Section 11); clear, unambiguous Decision-ownership language for the single most important user action in the product (Section 6); a mature Figma-governance and package-architecture model at the final-assembly layer (Sections 16–17); and a document culture that frequently and honestly names its own open questions rather than papering over them (Sections 4, 12) — which is precisely what made large parts of this review possible to write with confidence rather than speculation.

**Major weaknesses:** a core information-hierarchy contradiction between two documents in an explicit supersession relationship (C-1); a genuine, values-relevant contradiction in how AI-generated content is or is not silently converted to user-authored content (C-2); three-way disagreement on the Decision Workspace's own section order and completion-gate rules (C-3, C-4); a demonstrated pattern of self-audit claims that do not survive independent verification (Finding 8.2, Finding 9.1); and — the most structurally serious issue — a large fraction of the "final," most implementation-ready component-library document resting on two source specifications that are absent from the repository and cannot currently be verified by anything else in it (C-5).

**Highest-priority improvements:** resolve the five Critical/Mandatory findings in Section 23 as explicit, dated architectural decisions before the corresponding implementation work begins; treat every existing "final assembly" or "self-audit" claim in the set as provisional until independently spot-checked, given this review found at least two such claims to be inaccurate on direct inspection; and put a process in place (even a lightweight one) that would have caught the UX-013C/013D gap before UX-013E was written depending on it.

**Architectural grade: C+ (Conditionally Ready).**

**Confidence in this assessment:** High for every finding traced to a direct quotation (the large majority of this review); explicitly lower, and stated as such throughout, for anything this review could only evaluate via UX-013E's own secondhand account of an absent source document.

**Is Atlas's UX documentation suitable as the long-term foundation?** Conditionally — the philosophical and structural bones are sound enough to build on, and nothing found in this review requires redesigning the product or its Workspace model. But "long-term foundation" implies the component library and design system underneath that philosophy are trustworthy enough to extend without re-litigating their own basic facts, and today they are not: an implementer cannot currently determine, from the documents alone, how Challenges should be visually weighted, in what order the Decision Workspace's sections run, how many fields gate its most important action, or whether half of its component families were specified from real source material. These are fixable, and the fixes are narrow and named in this review — but they are not yet fixed.

**Should detailed component implementation begin now?** **Partially.** Foundation-tier (UX-013A) implementation can reasonably begin now, in parallel with resolving the Mandatory items, since its provenance is sound and its remaining issues (Finding 8.2, token-identity mismatch) are Medium/High but not gating. Reasoning-tier (UX-013B) implementation should wait for the naming/classification resolutions in Section 22's recommendations 1–3 (hierarchy, section order are directly load-bearing for Reasoning components) but does not need to wait for the UX-013C/013D gap to close. **Decision-, Monitoring-, and AI-Collaboration-tier implementation should not begin** until either the missing source documents are produced or their specific claims in UX-013E are independently re-derived and re-verified — building the product's core commitment mechanism on an unverifiable secondhand specification is the single highest-cost mistake this review can identify a clear, avoidable path around.
